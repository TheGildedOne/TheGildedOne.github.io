In May 1932 a work crew on the acropolis of Cumae, on the coast north-west of Naples, dug the fill out of a passage that had been blocked since antiquity. What opened in front of them was a corridor cut straight into the volcanic rock: about 131 metres long, nearly five metres high, and shaped in cross-section like a keyhole, wide at the floor and tapering to a flat ceiling. Down one flank, six openings let in slabs of daylight. At the far end was a vaulted room.

The archaeologist in charge, Amedeo Maiuri, was carrying a text in his head. He knew Book 6 of the Aeneid, where Aeneas reaches Cumae and finds the Sibyl's cave: a vast cavern hewn from the flank of the Euboean rock, approached by a hundred wide mouths, a hundred entrances, out of which rush as many voices, the answers of the Sibyl. Maiuri looked at his hundred-odd metres of corridor with light coming in along the side, and called it the Antro della Sibilla, the Cave of the Sibyl. It has been labelled that on every map since.

The woman who asked for the wrong thing

In the tradition, the Cumaean Sibyl was a prophetess of Apollo who had been at Cumae more or less forever. Ancient writers counted as many as ten Sibyls scattered round the Mediterranean, but the Cumaean was the one Rome kept in its founding story, because she was the old woman who sold the Sibylline Books to the last of the kings. She offered nine at a steep price, was laughed off, burned three and offered six for the same money, was refused again, burned three more, and got her original price for the last three. Rome shelved them in the Capitol and opened them by vote of the Senate for the next five centuries.

Ovid gives her the detail everyone remembers. Apollo offered her anything she wanted for her love. She scooped up a handful of sand and asked for as many birthdays as there were grains, and forgot to ask that the years be young ones. By the time Aeneas meets her she has centuries still to run and is already withering. A couple of generations after Virgil, Petronius has a dinner guest claim he saw her with his own eyes at Cumae, hung up in a jar, and that when the local boys shouted "Sibyl, what do you want?" she said: I want to die.

A hundred mouths, or six windows

The trouble with Maiuri's identification is that Virgil was writing poetry, not filing a site report. The hundred mouths are an image for the many-voiced roar of prophecy. He never says he measured anything. And yet the physical fit is unnerving: a gallery cut in the rock, a rank of openings along one side, a room at the end.

Then the dating caught up with the romance. The masonry and the pottery put the gallery in the fourth or early third century BCE. That is Hellenistic work, generations after any Sibyl a Roman would have recognised, and two centuries before Virgil described the place. The keyhole cross-section turns out not to be mystical at all: it is a standard Greek way of cutting a gallery that will not collapse, and there are others of the same profile at Cumae and around the bay. The side openings run along a defensive terrace. Some of the side chambers held cisterns. The reading most archaeologists now prefer is flat and practical: the corridor is a military work, a covered passage guarding the western side of the acropolis, with the end chamber cut or recut later.

Neither proven nor knocked down

What nobody can do is show that it was never a cult place, or never became one. Cumae kept a Sibyl in its civic identity for centuries, which means there was presumably a spot on the ground that went with her, and no one has found a better candidate on the hill. A tunnel dug for defence can be repurposed. The end chamber, with its cut benches and basins, has been read as somewhere a prophetess could have received people who came to ask. The honest position is the awkward one. The most famous oracular cave in the world is a Hellenistic tunnel that may or may not be the Sibyl's cave, and the evidence that would decide it does not exist.

It is the same move that produced the Oracle of the Dead in Epirus, where an excavator matched a large building to a scene in Homer, published it as the entrance to the underworld, and had the label stick for thirty years before other archaeologists started pointing at the farmhouse fittings. A famous text plus a suggestive ruin is a powerful combination, and a hard one to walk back once the signage is up.

Reading the guidebook backwards

There is a loop in it worth sitting with. Maiuri found the cave by using Virgil as a guide. But Virgil finished the Aeneid around 19 BCE, by which point the gallery, whatever it had been built for, had stood on that hill for roughly three hundred years. If it was a known feature of Cumae, Virgil could have walked through it, or been told about it, and folded it into the poem. In that case Maiuri did not establish that the corridor was the Sibyl's cave. He established, at most, that Virgil had also been to Cumae.

The Sibyl herself, if she sat anywhere, sat in a world closer to the Pythia's: a real woman, in a real room, answering real questions for a fee. The sign at the entrance still says Antro della Sibilla. Inside, the light still comes through the six openings in hard bars across the floor, exactly as it did for the men with shovels in 1932.
