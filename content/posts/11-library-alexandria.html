Everyone knows the story. There was a library in Alexandria holding all the knowledge of the ancient world. Someone burned it. Depending on which version reaches you, the someone is Julius Caesar, a Christian mob, or a Muslim caliph. Because of that fire, civilisation lost centuries.

Essentially every part of that is wrong, and the correction isn't pedantry. What actually happened is a more useful story, and a considerably bleaker one.

What the place actually was

Not a building full of books that people visited. It was part of the Mouseion — a shrine of the Muses — a royal research institution founded under the first Ptolemies in the early third century BCE.

Resident scholars drew a salary from the crown, were housed and fed, exempted from taxes, ate together, and had no teaching obligations. In modern terms: a state-funded institute for advanced study. The library was its working collection.

Acquisitions were famously aggressive. Ships docking at Alexandria were reportedly searched for books, which were copied — sometimes with the copy returned and the original kept.

The output justified it. This is where Eratosthenes calculated the circumference of the Earth to within a few percent, where Aristarchus proposed a heliocentric model eighteen centuries before Copernicus, where the text of Homer was edited into roughly the form we read, and where Euclid probably worked.

The fires, one at a time

48 BCE, Caesar. During the Alexandrian War his forces fired ships in the harbour and the blaze spread ashore. Agreed. But Cassius Dio specifies that what burned were storehouses near the docks holding grain and books — export stock awaiting shipment, not the Mouseion's collection. The decisive evidence is Strabo, who visited about twenty-five years later, describes the Mouseion in the present tense with its covered walk and communal dining hall, and mentions no catastrophe. He was standing in it.

272 CE, Aurelian. Under-discussed, and probably the one that mattered. Suppressing Zenobia's revolt, Aurelian's forces devastated the Brucheion — the palace quarter containing the Mouseion. The district was largely destroyed and never fully recovered. If a single military event ended the institution, this is the best candidate, and almost nobody has heard of it.

391 CE, the Serapeum. Bishop Theophilus led the destruction of the temple of Serapis, which had housed a subsidiary collection. This genuinely happened. But Ammianus Marcellinus, writing shortly before, already refers to the libraries there in the past tense.

415 CE, Hypatia. Not a library event at all. Hypatia was a distinguished mathematician murdered by a mob in a vicious episode of Alexandrian factional violence. Her death gets narrated as the end of the Library, which is a modern conflation. She wasn't a librarian, and there was no functioning Library left to be the last of.

642 CE, the Caliph Omar. The famous tale — the books heated the city's bathhouses for six months, on the grounds that they either agreed with the Quran or contradicted it — first appears roughly six centuries after the conquest. No contemporary trace. Rejected by essentially all specialists. It has the shape of a polemical anecdote because that's what it is.

The fire story survives for the same reason it's false: it converts a diffuse structural failure into a single dramatic act with someone to blame. Every version has been useful to someone with an argument to make about a particular group.

What actually killed it

Three unglamorous causes, all still recognisable.

The funding stopped. The Mouseion existed because Ptolemaic kings paid for it as dynastic prestige. When that logic weakened, so did the institution. In 145 BCE Ptolemy VIII purged Alexandria's intellectuals in a factional dispute and scholars fled to Rhodes, Pergamon and Athens. Under Rome it continued, but as a shadow, with appointments increasingly handed out as sinecures.

Papyrus doesn't last. This is the point most people miss and it's the central one. Papyrus in Alexandria's damp coastal climate degrades within a couple of centuries at best. A library on papyrus isn't a store of books — it's a copying operation that has to run continuously. Every text needs recopying every few generations or it stops existing. Stop paying the scribes and the collection dissolves on its own, silently, with nobody setting fire to anything.

Attention moved. Pergamon built a rival library. Rome accumulated collections. Later Constantinople became the centre of gravity for Greek texts, and it was Byzantine scribes who decided, by choosing what to recopy, what would reach us.

What we actually lost

Here the myth undersells the disaster.

Sophocles wrote around 120 plays. We have seven. Aeschylus wrote perhaps ninety. We have seven. Of Aristotle's published works — written for a general audience and praised in antiquity for their style — we have essentially none; what survives is lecture notes. Most of Sappho is gone. Livy wrote 142 books of Roman history; 35 survive. The bulk of Hellenistic science and mathematics is lost.

None of it happened in an afternoon. It happened because at some point, for each of those texts, the last person who was going to copy it decided not to — or died first, or couldn't find a patron, or found the papyrus already too rotted to read.

Surviving classical literature is estimated at somewhere around one percent of what once existed. That figure isn't the product of arson. It's a filter that ran for two thousand years, dropping anything nobody was actively maintaining.

The part that should worry you

Everything else on this site is downstream of the same mechanism. A cult whose doctrine vanished completely. An oracle with not one authenticated surviving line. The mysteries weren't only kept secret. They also, simply, weren't copied.

Knowledge isn't stored. It's maintained — and maintenance is a recurring cost some institution has to keep choosing to pay. When the payments stop, nothing dramatic happens. The material just becomes unreadable over a period longer than anyone's attention span, and by the time the loss is visible there's nobody left who knows what was in it.

No villain required. That's the disturbing bit.
