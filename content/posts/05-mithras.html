Somewhere between the second and fourth centuries CE, men across the Roman Empire went down into small artificial caves, sat facing one another on benches, and looked at a picture.

It was always the same picture. A young man in a Phrygian cap kneels on a bull, hauls its head back by the nostrils, and drives a short knife into its neck. He isn't watching — his face is turned away, over his shoulder. A dog leaps at the wound. A snake stretches toward it. A scorpion grips the bull's testicles. A raven sits above, watching.

We have hundreds of these, from Syria to the Rhine to a fort on Hadrian's Wall. The iconography is more standardised than almost anything else in Roman religion — a Mithraist from Dura-Europos would have walked into a temple in Britain and recognised it instantly.

Nobody knows what it means, because not one member ever wrote it down.

What the archaeology hands us free

More than you'd expect, actually.

The buildings. A mithraeum is small, rectangular, half-buried or built to feel that way, with benches down both sides. Capacity twenty to forty. This isn't a religion of congregations — it's a religion of cells, and spreading it meant founding hundreds of them.

The membership. Dedications name soldiers, centurions, freedmen in imperial administration, customs officials, merchants. No women appear anywhere in the epigraphic record. The cult travelled the roads and the army lists.

The grades. Seven ranks: Raven, Bridegroom, Soldier, Lion, Persian, Sun-Runner, Father. Not reconstruction — a mosaic floor at Ostia lays them out in order with their symbols, and painted inscriptions at Santa Prisca back it up. Each carries a planetary association.

The meal. Those benches are for reclining and eating. Bones, cups and cooking debris confirm shared meals as routine practice.

So: a graded, all-male, cell-based initiatory society with a fixed cult image, a shared meal and a planetary scheme. Plenty of structure. Zero content.

The theory that ruled for seventy years, then died

Franz Cumont catalogued the material in the 1890s and supplied the explanation: Roman Mithraism was Iranian Mithra worship carried west by soldiers and traders, its Zoroastrian content adapted but continuous.

It was coherent, it explained the Persian costume, and it held almost unchallenged into the 1970s.

Then it collapsed, on a simple objection: the bull-slaying at the centre of Roman Mithraism does not occur in Iranian religion. Neither do the seven grades. Neither does the cave-temple. What Rome had was a name, a hat, and an origin story.

Which is revealing in itself. The Persian branding looks like a claim to ancient foreign authority, made by a cult that was in fact a Roman invention of the imperial period. Ancient religions did this more often than we assume.

The star map

With Iran gone, the field needed a new explanation. The most striking came from David Ulansey in 1989, and it's hard to unsee once you've seen it.

Take the figures in the tauroctony and look for them in the sky:

  • The bull — Taurus
  • The dog — Canis Minor
  • The snake — Hydra
  • The scorpion — Scorpius
  • The raven — Corvus
  • The cup that appears in many versions — Crater
  • The ear of grain at the bull's tail — Spica, the bright star in Virgo

Every figure has a constellation, and they sit in a coherent group. The tauroctony is a star chart.

Ulansey then asks what event it records, and this is the ambitious part. Around 128 BCE Hipparchus is credited with recognising the precession of the equinoxes — the slow wobble that drags the stars against the seasons over millennia. That shift had recently moved the spring equinox out of Taurus. The age of the bull was over.

A god who can kill the bull, on this reading, is a god who can move the entire celestial sphere — a power above the cosmos itself. And directly above Taurus sits Perseus, conventionally depicted in a Phrygian cap, holding a short blade, face turned away from what he kills.

Why it isn't settled

It's elegant, which is exactly what should make you careful.

Precession is a subtle technical discovery, and asking whether frontier centurions sustained a mystery religion built on Hipparchan astronomy is a fair question with no good answer. The Perseus identification is argued rather than attested — no ancient source connects them. And the night sky is crowded enough that a determined reader can find a constellation for most things.

Roger Beck's version holds up better. Rather than one specific astronomical event, Beck reads the mithraeum itself as a working model of the cosmos, with the grades mapping a soul's ascent through the planetary spheres. Its advantage is outside support: Porphyry, writing in the third century, states plainly that the Mithraic cave was built as an image of the universe, and discusses celestial gates that souls pass through.

Porphyry wasn't an initiate. But he was a serious philosopher with access to informants, and he's the closest thing to a friendly witness the cult has.

Why there's nothing left

Consider the scale of what's missing. Around four hundred mithraea are known or suspected. Each had a founding, a hierarchy and an initiation, and seven ranks imply a teaching sequence — you can't promote a man through grades without something to promote him for. Multiply that across three centuries and an empire. The volume of doctrine that once existed was enormous. The surviving quantity, written by a Mithraist, is zero.

Two explanations, not mutually exclusive.

The first is destruction. Several mithraea show deliberate violence in the fourth century — cult images smashed, statues decapitated, sanctuaries filled in. A religion of small hidden rooms with no public institutions has very little resilience against that.

The second is that there may never have been much to destroy. The grades, the image, the cave and the meal are all ways of transmitting meaning without text. If your doctrine is taught by walking a man through seven stages of a room built as a model of the sky, you may not need books — and when the rooms are filled in and the last Father dies, it stops completely.

Which leaves us standing in the best-preserved secret rooms in the ancient world, in front of a picture we can describe perfectly and cannot read.
