Somewhere between the second and fourth centuries CE, men across the Roman Empire went underground into small artificial caves, sat facing each other on benches down two long walls, and looked at a picture.

The picture was always the same. A young man in a Phrygian cap kneels on the back of a bull. He pulls its head back by the nostrils and drives a short knife into its neck. He is not looking at the bull — his face is turned away, over his shoulder. A dog leaps up to the wound. A snake stretches toward it. A scorpion grips the bull's testicles. A raven sits above. Two smaller figures flank the scene with torches, one raised, one lowered.

We have hundreds of these. They come from Syria, from the Rhine, from Rome, from the Danube frontier, from London, from a fort on Hadrian's Wall. The iconography is more standardised than almost anything else in Roman religion — a Mithraist from Dura-Europos would have recognised the image in a temple in Britain immediately.

And we have no idea what it means, because nobody inside the cult ever wrote it down.

What the archaeology gives us for free

Before the theories, the solid ground. It is more substantial than people expect.

The buildings. A mithraeum is small, rectangular, half-buried or built to feel so, with a vaulted ceiling and benches along both sides. Capacity is typically twenty to forty. This is not a religion of congregations. It is a religion of cells, and its spread required founding hundreds of them rather than building anything large.

The membership. Dedicatory inscriptions name soldiers, centurions, freedmen in imperial administration, customs officials and merchants. No women appear anywhere in the epigraphic record as initiates. The cult travelled the roads and the army lists.

The grades. Seven ranks in ascending order: Raven, Bridegroom, Soldier, Lion, Persian, Sun-Runner, Father. This is not reconstruction — a mosaic floor at Ostia lays them out in sequence with their symbols, and painted inscriptions at Santa Prisca in Rome corroborate it. Each grade carries a planetary association, which matters for what follows.

The meal. The benches are for reclining and eating. Bones, cups and cooking debris recovered from mithraea confirm shared meals as a regular part of practice.

So: a graded, all-male, cell-based initiatory society with a fixed cult image, a shared meal, and a planetary scheme. That is a great deal of structure. What is missing is every word of what they believed.

The theory that dominated for seventy years, and fell

Franz Cumont assembled the first great catalogue of Mithraic material in the 1890s, and supplied the interpretation that went with it: Roman Mithraism was Iranian Mithra worship, carried west by soldiers and traders, its Zoroastrian content adapted but essentially continuous.

It was a coherent story, it explained the Persian dress in the images, and it held the field almost unchallenged into the 1970s.

It is now largely abandoned. The critique that dismantled it, argued forcefully at the first international Mithraic congress in 1971 and after, was straightforward: the bull-slaying scene that stands at the centre of Roman Mithraism does not occur in Iranian religion. The seven grades do not occur there. The cave-temple does not occur there. What Rome had was a name, a hat, and an origin story — and almost nothing else that can be traced east.

Which is itself revealing. The Persian branding looks like a claim to antiquity and exotic authority, made by a cult that was in fact a Roman invention of the imperial period. Ancient religions did this more often than we tend to assume.

The star map

With Iran removed, the field needed a new explanation for the image, and the most striking came from David Ulansey in 1989.

His starting observation is hard to dismiss once you have seen it. Take the elements of the tauroctony and look for them in the sky along a single band of constellations:

  • The bull — Taurus
  • The dog — Canis Minor
  • The snake — Hydra
  • The scorpion — Scorpius
  • The raven — Corvus
  • The cup that appears in many versions — Crater
  • The ear of grain often shown at the bull's tail — Spica, the bright star in Virgo

Every figure in the scene corresponds to a constellation, and they lie in a coherent group. The tauroctony, on this reading, is a star chart.

Ulansey then asks what event the chart records, and his answer is the ambitious part. Around 128 BCE Hipparchus is credited with recognising the precession of the equinoxes — the slow wobble that shifts the position of the stars against the seasons over millennia. That shift had, not long before, moved the spring equinox out of Taurus. The age of the bull had ended.

A god who could kill the bull, Ulansey argues, is a god capable of moving the entire celestial sphere — a power greater than the cosmos itself. And Mithras is placed directly above Taurus in the sky, in the position of Perseus, who is depicted with a Phrygian cap, a short blade, and his face turned away from what he kills.

Why it is not settled

The theory is elegant, and elegance is exactly what should make one careful.

The problems are these. Precession is a subtle technical discovery; asking whether frontier centurions on the Danube sustained a mystery religion built on Hipparchan astronomy is a fair question, and the honest answer is that we do not know. The Perseus identification is argued rather than attested — no ancient source connects them. And there is a selection issue: the night sky is crowded, and a determined reader can find a constellation for most things.

Roger Beck's work offers a more defensible version of the astronomical case. Rather than one specific astronomical event, Beck reads the mithraeum itself as a working model of the cosmos, within which the grades map a soul's ascent through the planetary spheres. The great advantage of this reading is that it has outside support: Porphyry, writing in the third century, states plainly that the Mithraic cave was designed as an image of the universe, and discusses celestial gates through which souls pass.

Porphyry was not an initiate. But he was a serious philosopher with access to informants, and he is the closest thing to a friendly witness the cult has.

The scale of what is missing

Roughly four hundred mithraea are known or suspected. Each had a founding, a hierarchy, a liturgy and instruction — the seven grades imply a teaching sequence, because you cannot promote someone through ranks without content to promote them for. Multiply that across three centuries and the Empire, and the volume of doctrine that once existed was enormous. The surviving quantity, written by a Mithraist, is zero.

Why nothing survived

Two explanations, and they are not exclusive.

The first is that Mithraism was destroyed rather than faded. Several mithraea show deliberate violent damage in the fourth century — cult images smashed, statues decapitated, sanctuaries filled in. At least one contains human remains suggesting the destruction was not merely architectural. A cult of small hidden rooms and no public institutions has little resilience against that.

The second is that there may never have been much to destroy. The grades, the image, the cave and the meal are all mechanisms for transmitting meaning without text. If your doctrine is taught by walking a man through seven stages of a room built as a model of the sky, you may simply not need books — and when the rooms are filled in and the last Father dies, the transmission stops completely.

That leaves us where we are: standing in the best-preserved secret rooms in the ancient world, in front of an image we can describe in perfect detail and cannot read.
