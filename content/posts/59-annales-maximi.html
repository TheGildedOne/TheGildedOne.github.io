Outside the chief priest's house in the Roman Forum stood a board, painted white.

Each year the pontifex maximus wrote on it: who held office, and what had happened that the gods might care about. A shortage of grain. A darkening of the sun. A shower of stones, a lamb born with two heads, a statue struck by lightning. Anyone could stop and read it. Cicero says that was the point, so the people could know.

At the end of the year the board came down and the record was kept. Over centuries that made a chronicle of the Republic, written by its priests, year by year. Later Romans called it the annales maximi, the greatest annals.

What survives is a few scattered scraps, quoted by other writers.

How often grain was dear

Not everyone admired it. Cato the Elder, writing his own history of Rome in the second century BCE, refused to copy it. He didn't want to write, he said, what was on the chief priest's tablet: how often grain was dear, how often darkness or something else blocked the light of the moon or the sun.

It's the sneer of a man who wanted battles and speeches, and it tells us what was on the board. Food prices, because a shortage was a public crisis. Eclipses, because the sky going dark was a sign.

And prodigies. When something unnatural happened, the senate heard the reports and decided which needed public expiation, sometimes after consulting the Sibylline Books. Livy's history is full of yearly lists of them: a cow that spoke, blood in a spring, a wolf in the city. For a long time scholars took those lists as copied more or less straight from the priests' records.

A statue in the Comitium

One story survives with a book number attached. Aulus Gellius says he found it in book eleven of the annales maximi.

The statue of Horatius Cocles, the hero who held the bridge against the Etruscans, was struck by lightning. Rome sent for diviners from Etruria to tell them how to make amends, since lightning was the Etruscans' speciality. The diviners, who hated Rome, gave false advice on purpose. They said the statue should be moved down to a low spot the sun never reached.

The trick was found out. The diviners confessed and were executed. The statue went up to a high place instead, near the shrine of Vulcan, and things went well for Rome. Gellius adds that the city's boys went round chanting a line: bad advice is worst for the one who gives it.

It's an odd thing to find in a priests' chronicle. Either the board had room for a good story, or Gellius was reading something more elaborate than a whitewashed list.

Eighty books, or not

Cicero says the practice ran from the beginning of Rome down to the time of Publius Mucius Scaevola, who became pontifex maximus around 130 BCE. A late commentary on Virgil, the one that goes under the name of Servius, says the annals filled eighty books.

Put those together and you get the textbook version: Scaevola stopped the board and published the whole archive in eighty volumes around 120 BCE.

Bruce Frier, in 1979, pointed out how thin that is. Cicero says when the board stopped, not that anybody published anything. The eighty books turn up only in the Virgil commentary, centuries later. Frier argued the edition, if it existed in that form, was more probably a huge reworking done in the age of Augustus, when Rome's past was being tidied up in all sorts of ways. Not everyone accepted it. But nobody has produced the evidence that would settle it.

Elizabeth Rawson had already taken apart the other prop in 1971. The prodigy lists in Livy, she showed, were so rewritten and muddled that it was far from clear they came from the annales maximi at all.

Before the fire

There's a deeper problem at the start of the run. Livy says most of Rome's records were destroyed when the Gauls burned the city around 390 BCE. So whatever the eighty books held for the first three and a half centuries had to come from somewhere else.

Cicero gives a clue. He quotes the poet Ennius on an eclipse, around 400 BCE: on the Nones of June, the moon and night blocked the sun. That eclipse, he says, was in Ennius and in the annales maximi. And from it, earlier eclipses had been calculated backwards, all the way to one in the reign of Romulus.

Calculated. In other words someone worked out from astronomy what the sky must have done, and that went into the record of Rome's earliest years. Tim Cornell, among others, thinks some genuinely early material may have got through all the same. How much, we don't know.

What we do have is a line about grain, an eclipse, and a statue that got moved twice. And, somewhere in book eleven, a rhyme chanted by children in the street.
