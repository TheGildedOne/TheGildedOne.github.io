Once a year, a member of the Iobacchoi drew a lot. If it fell his way, he became Dionysos for the night. Others around the table became Kore, Palaimon, Aphrodite, and someone called Proteurythmos, whose name nobody has satisfactorily translated to this day. They ate their share of the sacrifice in character, in a private hall on the slopes below the Areopagus, and by morning they were themselves again.

We know this because the club wrote its rulebook onto a marble column and left it standing where members could read it on the way in. It survives complete: 163 lines, dated to 164 or 165 CE, the fullest set of by-laws any Dionysiac association left behind anywhere in the Greek world.

A club that ran on a schedule

Joining cost fifty denarii and a libation, unless your father had already been a member, in which case you paid twenty-five until you came of age. The Iobacchoi met on the ninth of every month, at the annual festival, on the Bacchic days proper, and whenever else the god's calendar called for it. Everyone owed fixed monthly dues, paid in wine, and the treasurer kept the books, supplied the lamp oil, and chased down anyone behind on payment.

Getting in was not automatic. A candidate had to register with the priest first and then survive a vote of the whole membership, admitted only, in the club's own phrase, "if he appears worthy and suitable." What made someone worthy or suitable to drink with the Iobacchoi is not spelled out. The vote itself was the test.

None of this is exotic on its own. It is the administrative skeleton of any club that has ever needed a treasurer. What makes the stone remarkable is that this particular club was organised around Dionysos, a god whose worship elsewhere in the Greek and Roman world could mean anything from a quiet wine offering to the kind of unsupervised night gathering that got the whole cult banned across Italy in 186 BCE. The Iobacchoi's own answer to that reputation was minutes and a treasurer. Most accounts of ancient mystery cults promise a look inside and rarely deliver one. This is the rare document that actually does, on an ordinary Tuesday rather than at the height of the rite.

The wand, the horses, and the price of a punch

Meetings did not run themselves. An officer called the eukosmos, keeper of order, carried the god's own wand, the thyrsos, and used it to point out anyone causing a disturbance. If the man refused to leave, the club kept enforcers on standing appointment for exactly this purpose, referred to in the text only as "the horses." They put him out.

The tariff for bad behaviour reads like a parking schedule. Insulting another member cost twenty-five light drachmas, payable to the treasury before readmission. Striking someone cost the same, or exclusion until you paid. Skipping a mandatory assembly cost fifty. Speaking out of turn, without the priest's leave, cost thirty. Somebody, clearly, had done all of these things before the rule was thought necessary, and the club had stopped being surprised by any of it.

The priest who may have been the richest man in Rome

The name presiding over all this is where the document stops being generic. The priest is recorded only as Claudius Herodes, and the standard scholarly identification, never stated outright on the stone itself, is Tiberius Claudius Herodes Atticus: sophist, senator, consul in 143 CE, and one of the wealthiest private individuals in the entire Roman Empire, a man who could fund an entire stadium in Athens from his own pocket and still have an estate to spare.

If the identification is right, and most epigraphers now accept it, then the man legislating fines for drunken scuffles in a Dionysiac drinking club was also a Roman senator who dined with emperors. The inscription feels no need to remark on this. Membership of the Iobacchoi ran across whatever social range a fifty-denarii fee could reach, and evidently that range stretched all the way to the top.

What forty years bought

One clause names an ordinary member directly: Aurelius Nikomachos, who had served the club as vice-priest for seventeen years and then as priest for twenty-three, forty years of unpaid religious office inside a private society most of Athens would never have heard of. When he finally stepped down, the inscription records that he inaugurated a new custom: from then on, the priest would give a discourse about the god, a theologia, at each gathering.

Nobody wrote down what Nikomachos actually said across forty years of service, or what the first theologia covered. The stone records only that the tradition started with him and was to continue. That is the pattern with the Iobacchoi throughout: exact figures for the fines, exact days for the meetings, exact portions of meat for whoever drew Aphrodite that year, and total silence on what a member believed while wearing the god's part for a night. What he actually said, in forty years of unpaid service to men who occasionally punched each other over a seating dispute, went with him.
