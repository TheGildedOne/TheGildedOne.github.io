The man who initiated Eunapius at Eleusis told him it was going to end. Not eventually, not in some vague coming darkness, but soon enough that he would live to see it: the temples pulled down, the worship of the two Goddesses finished, and the whole thing over before he himself was dead.

He was right on every count. Eunapius wrote it down afterwards, which is exactly the problem with it.

The ending had already happened on paper

The story people remember is the burning: Alaric comes through Greece with an army, reaches Eleusis, and two thousand years of continuous initiation stop in an afternoon.

That is roughly what happened, and it is the least important part.

By the time any Goth got near Attica, the mysteries were already illegal. Theodosius had spent the early 390s legislating them out of existence: sacrifice prohibited, temples closed, the whole apparatus of traditional public cult moved from the category of civic duty to the category of crime. The laws survive in the Theodosian Code and they are not ambiguous.

A mystery cult cannot run without sacrifice. Ban that and you have not persuaded anyone of anything, but you have shut the machine down.

So the sanctuary Alaric found was not a functioning institution he interrupted. It was a site that had been legally dead for several years, staffed by people continuing something the state had criminalised.

The priesthood broke before the building did

There is a second ending, and Eunapius cares about it far more than the fire.

The hierophant was supposed to come from the Eumolpidae, the Athenian family that had held the office for as long as anyone could count. The man who initiated Eunapius was of that line. Eunapius declines to give his name, on the grounds that he initiated him personally.

His successor was not. Eunapius is scathing: the man was a citizen of Thespiae, not even an Athenian, and he was a priest of Mithras. He had taken the most senior office in the most prestigious cult in Greece without any of the qualifications the office required, and Eunapius regarded the appointment as a violation of oaths.

Strip out the sneering and something real is left. The succession had failed. There was no legitimate candidate, or nobody willing, and the post went to an outsider from a different religion because the alternative was leaving it empty.

That is what institutional death actually looks like. Not a dramatic destruction, but a hiring problem.

Our only witness, and his grudge

Everything above comes from one man, and he is not a neutral one.

Eunapius was an initiate, a Greek intellectual, and a committed enemy of Christianity, writing about the destruction of his own religious world by people he despised. His account of Alaric's arrival includes the detail that gets quoted most: that the way into Greece was opened by the impiety of the men in black raiment, who came in alongside the invaders unhindered.

The men in black are monks. He is accusing them of collaborating with a barbarian army against the sanctuaries.

Treat that as what it is. There is no corroboration for it. It is a polemical charge by a hostile writer, in a passage designed to make the reader feel that something sacred was betrayed from within. Monks were certainly involved in destroying temples elsewhere in this period, which is why the charge is plausible enough to have survived; plausible is not the same as attested.

The prophecy needs the same treatment. A prediction recorded only after its fulfilment, by the initiate of the man who supposedly made it, is not evidence about the future. It is evidence about how Eunapius wanted the ending to read: foreseen, inevitable, and a judgement rather than an accident.

None of that means he is lying about the events. It means the shape he gives them is his.

What actually stopped

It is worth being concrete about the scale, because the numbers are easy to skim past.

Eleusis had been initiating people annually for something close to two millennia, in a hall built to hold thousands at once. Hundreds of thousands of people had gone through it. Emperors were initiated. It outlasted the Athenian empire, Alexander, the Roman Republic and most of the Roman Empire.

And the central rite was never written down by anyone who saw it. That was still true on the last day.

So the ending is unusually clean, in the worst way. There was no final account, no summarising document, no initiate who broke the silence once the penalty for breaking it had stopped existing. Everything argued since about what happened in that hall has been argued from the outside, and the sack closed the last chance of it being otherwise.

A tidier story than the evidence supports

The version that gets told is a single dramatic night: the last mystery cult of the ancient world, extinguished by a barbarian army, the pagan world ending in fire.

The evidence gives something slower and less satisfying. Laws that made the rites impossible. A priesthood that ran out of qualified priests and filled the gap with a man from the wrong city and the wrong god. Attendance we cannot measure and probably would not be impressed by. Then, finally, an army passing through a country it was already ransacking, burning a site that had stopped legally operating.

Recent work on the period has pushed hard against the idea that classical religion ended in a series of violent set pieces at all, and Eleusis fits that pattern better than it fits the dramatic version. The fire was real. It was also the last event in a sequence that had already finished the job.

The man who told Eunapius it was coming had it right, and probably did not need a god to see it.
