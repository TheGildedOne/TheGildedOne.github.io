Somewhere in Sogdiana, in what is now Uzbekistan, Alexander's army came to a small town whose people spoke Greek. Badly, by now, mixed with the local language, but Greek. They came out to meet the Macedonians with garlands and handed the town over. According to the Roman historian Curtius Rufus, Alexander asked who they were, and was told: descendants of the Branchidae, the family that once ran Apollo's oracle at Didyma.

Curtius says Alexander let the Milesians in his army decide what to do with them. They couldn't agree. So the next day he had the town surrounded and everyone in it killed. The walls were pulled down. The sacred groves were cut, and the roots dug out, so that not even the ground would be left.

That is the story. Whether it happened is another matter.

A family firm

Didyma sat about twenty kilometres south of Miletus, on the coast of Ionia, linked to the city by a sacred road. For most of the archaic period it wasn't really a Milesian sanctuary at all. It belonged, in practice, to the Branchidae, a clan who claimed descent from Branchus, a shepherd boy Apollo fell in love with and gave the gift of prophecy. Greeks called the whole place Branchidae after them.

It was a serious rival to Delphi. Herodotus says Croesus of Lydia sent Didyma offerings equal in weight to what he sent to Delphi, and he tells a sharp little story about the people of Cyme asking Didyma whether to hand over a fugitive to the Persians. The god said yes. A sceptical envoy named Aristodicus started evicting the sparrows nesting around the temple, and a voice from the inner shrine asked how he dared drive out its suppliants. Aristodicus asked why, then, the god was ordering Cyme to surrender theirs. So that you'll be destroyed faster, came the reply, and stop asking about handing over suppliants.

Where the treasure went

Then Ionia revolted against Persia, and lost. Here the sources split for the first time. Herodotus says that after Miletus fell in 494 BCE, the sanctuary at Didyma was plundered and burnt under Darius. Strabo, writing about five centuries later, says it was Xerxes, around 479, and adds the detail that matters: the Branchidae themselves handed the god's treasure over to the Persian king, then left with him to escape the consequences. Pausanias says Xerxes carried the temple's bronze Apollo off to Ecbatana.

Excavators have found burn marks on parts of the archaic temple, so something violent happened. But which king, which year, and whether the priests were traitors or just refugees on the losing side, the evidence doesn't settle. N. G. L. Hammond argued in 1998 for Strabo's Xerxes. Plenty of scholars still follow Herodotus, who was writing within living memory.

What is agreed is that the Branchidae disappear from Didyma. When the oracle comes back, it is run by the city of Miletus, which elected a prophet every year and carved the names on stone.

The massacre that one historian forgot

The Sogdiana story is the only other place the family surfaces. Strabo mentions it briefly: Xerxes had settled them in the east, and Alexander razed their town out of disgust at their ancestors' sacrilege. Curtius gives the full account. Diodorus clearly told it too, because the surviving table of contents for his book seventeen lists it, but that section is lost.

Arrian, who is usually the best of Alexander's historians and worked from the memoirs of men who were there, says nothing at all.

That silence has driven the argument for a century. W. W. Tarn, who admired Alexander intensely, thought the whole episode was invented. H. W. Parke, in a 1985 paper, pointed out that the story likely goes back to Callisthenes, Alexander's court historian and an eyewitness if it happened, and argued it was real. Others suspect the Milesian consultation was a later literary touch to make an atrocity look like justice. Punishing a whole town for something their great-great-grandparents did, 150 years earlier, is either the most brutal thing Alexander ever did or a good story someone told about him. We don't know which.

A spring that started again

Callisthenes is also the source of the oracle's revival, and it's suspiciously convenient. Strabo passes on his claim that Didyma had been silent since the Branchidae robbed it, its spring dry, and that when Alexander arrived the water began to flow again. Milesian envoys then brought oracles to Egypt confirming that Alexander was the son of Zeus. The court historian reports that the god has endorsed the king. The court historian may have been doing his job.

Whatever started it, the reborn oracle worked through a prophetess seated near that spring. Iamblichus, writing much later, says she either held a staff or sat on an axle, and took in the god by wetting her feet or breathing vapour from the water. Her answers, unlike the Pythia's at Delphi, were written down. It's the same kind of arrangement, water, darkness and a professional to put the words in order, that ran at Claros a short sail up the coast.

The temple they never finished

To house her, Miletus started one of the biggest temples in the Greek world: a double ring of more than a hundred Ionic columns, each nearly twenty metres high, around an inner courtyard open to the sky, with a small shrine and a laurel grove inside. Work began after 334 BCE and dragged on for around six hundred years. It was never completed. Some columns still have no fluting.

The builders left their working drawings behind. In 1979 the architectural historian Lothar Haselberger noticed faint lines etched into the courtyard walls: full-scale construction plans for the columns and mouldings, incised into the marble and never polished off, because the polishing never happened either.

Early in the fourth century, the emperor Diocletian sent to ask the god what to do about the Christians. Lactantius, a Christian writing soon afterwards, says the answer came back as the words of an enemy of their religion. The first edict against them followed in February 303. It was among the last questions Didyma is known to have answered, asked in a temple still waiting for its roof.
