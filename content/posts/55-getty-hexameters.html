In 1981 a Los Angeles doctor named Max Gerchik gave the Getty Museum some lead.

There were several pieces, all from Sicily and all inscribed. One was a sacred law from Selinous, now famous in its own right. Three were curses. And one was a thin sheet, about half the size of a page of letter paper, broken into six fragments and covered with Greek.

It took thirty years to publish. In the meantime scholars passed tracings around seminar rooms at Harvard, Columbia and UCLA, and argued. Richard Janko, who was at two of those seminars, noticed later that the early tracings show letters the tablet no longer has. Bits had dropped off along the breaks while everyone was thinking about it.

What the lead says

It's poetry. About fifty lines of hexameter, the metre of Homer, of which thirty or so can be followed.

It opens like a guarantee. Write these sacred verses on tin, hide them in a house of stone, and nothing bred on the broad earth or in the sea can harm you. (The copy we have is on lead. Nobody seems to have minded.)

Then a refrain to Paean, the healer, who sends protective drugs everywhere. It comes back four times and divides the text into sections.

The first section is a tiny, strange myth. A child leads a goat out of Persephone's garden, down from shadowy mountains, to be milked. The goat belongs to Demeter and is heavy with milk that never runs out. The goat follows goddesses carrying torches, and Hecate of the crossroads leads the way, screaming in a voice the poem calls hair-raising and foreign.

A later section turns practical. The verses are to be spoken for the people, for men at war and in ships, for flocks and for crafts, by night and by day. It ends on a line that amounts to a civic motto: this is better for the city.

And then the tablet says: aski kataski.

Seven hundred years of nonsense

Those are the first of the Ephesia grammata, the "Ephesian letters", the best-known magic words in Greek. Later writers quote them as gibberish that kept you safe.

On the Getty tablet they aren't quite gibberish. They sit inside a verse spell credited to the Idaean Dactyls, and the lines around them are about a goat driven from a garden to milking. It looks like the same little story as before, worn down until only the sound was left. Alberto Bernabé argues exactly that: the famous magic words began as a narrative and decayed.

The decay can be watched, because the Getty text has relatives. Versions of the same verses turn up on lead at Himera and at Selinous itself in the fifth century BCE, at Locri in southern Italy in the fourth, and at Phalasarna in Crete a little later. Then on a silver sheet from Rome, and finally on lead from Oxyrhynchus and in a spellbook now in Michigan, both of the third or fourth century CE, in the world of the Greek Magical Papyri. By then the copyists had no idea what they were copying. The late copies are badly garbled.

A spell, or a liturgy?

Here is the argument, and it's a real one.

David Jordan and Roy Kotansky, who deciphered the tablet and finally published it in 2011, called the verses the traditional words spoken at an initiation into the worship of Demeter and Kore. Dirk Obbink agreed: poetry performed in a mystery setting. William Furley went further and saw the child with the goat as the infant Dionysos, and the author as one of the wandering Orphic initiators Plato complains about. On that reading the tablet is a cousin of the gold tablets buried with initiates, several of which come from southern Italy and which also talk about Persephone and milk.

Janko's view is that the text has nothing whatever to do with mystery cult. It is an incantation against witchcraft, he says, with an older spell folded inside it, and it was meant for a city and not a private client. Christopher Faraone and Radcliffe Edmonds also read it as civic protection. Luigi Battezzato, reviewing the field in 2022, pointed out that the surviving lines never mention initiation at all. Persephone is there, and so is one of the Dactyls. An initiate isn't.

Ian Rutherford has a third label: a paean that is also a spell, the only one of its kind to survive.

Part of the trouble is that we sort these things into boxes the fifth century may not have owned. A poem that names Persephone and wards off harm could be recited at a rite and scratched on lead for the doorpost.

The missing piece of evidence

One fact would settle a good deal, and it's gone.

If the tablet came out of a sanctuary, the liturgy reading gains. From a house wall, it's an amulet doing what its first lines promise. From a grave, something else again. Selinous has produced a good deal of inscribed lead, curse tablets included, and three of those arrived at the Getty in the same gift.

But the Getty tablet wasn't excavated. It surfaced on the market, and Kotansky himself describes it as found in clandestine digging. Janko reports that it is thought to come from the sanctuary of Demeter Malophoros and Hecate at Selinous. That would be a perfect fit for the goddesses in the poem, which is rather the problem with it.

Even the city is in doubt. The first editors noticed that the tablet's sign for the h sound has its parallel at Himera, on the opposite coast, and not at Selinous. Battezzato thinks Himera the likelier home. Carthage destroyed Selinous in 409 BCE, and the usual dating leans on that.

The Getty's own label says fourth century. The lead is thin and fragile, and the tablet is kept in storage. It was taken out in 2010 so the people arguing about it could look.
