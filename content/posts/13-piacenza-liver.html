In 1877, a farmer ploughing a field near Settima, not far from Piacenza in northern Italy, turned up a piece of bronze about the size of a hand. It is shaped like a sheep's liver, complete with the lobes and the protruding processus pyramidalis, modelled accurately enough that a veterinarian would recognise the organ.

The flat underside is divided by incised lines into forty compartments — sixteen running around the perimeter, twenty-four arranged within. Each compartment carries an inscription: the abbreviated name of a god.

It is not decoration and it is not a votive offering. It is a piece of professional equipment, and it is the closest thing we have to a surviving instrument from a religious technology that the Roman state relied on for eight hundred years.

Reading the future in an animal

The Etruscans practised haruspicy — divination by inspecting the entrails of a sacrificed animal, with particular attention to the liver.

The choice is less arbitrary than it sounds. The liver is large, densely vascularised, and highly variable between individuals. It shows disease clearly, changes colour and texture readily, and presents an abundance of features to interpret: lobes, fissures, the gall bladder, marks and swellings. If you require a natural object with enough detail to yield a specific reading every time, it is a good choice. Mesopotamian diviners had reached the same conclusion centuries earlier, and clay liver models survive from Babylonia too.

The Etruscan system worked by correspondence. The liver was treated as a small-scale model of the cosmos. The sky was divided into regions, each belonging to a deity, and those regions were mapped onto the surface of the organ. A mark appearing in a given zone was therefore a communication from the god who owned the matching quarter of heaven — and its position told you both who was speaking and whether the message was favourable.

The bronze liver is the key to that mapping. It is a reference model: a physical lookup table, showing which god belongs where.

The sixteen regions, corroborated

The most important feature is the ring of sixteen divisions around the edge, because this is where the object connects to independent evidence.

Roman writers record that Etruscan doctrine divided the sky into sixteen parts for the interpretation of lightning — a system in which the direction from which a bolt came determined which god had sent it and what it meant. Pliny the Elder describes the scheme. Martianus Capella, writing much later, provides a list of the deities inhabiting the sixteen celestial regions.

The perimeter of the Piacenza Liver has sixteen divisions, and the names inscribed in them correspond, where they can be read, to the pattern those Roman sources describe.

That correspondence does two things. It confirms that Roman accounts of Etruscan religion were reporting a real system rather than inventing an exotic one. And it confirms what the object is for: the liver in the diviner's hands was a small copy of the sky above him, and reading one was reading the other.

The orientation problem

Note what this requires in practice. The haruspex had to hold the organ in a specific orientation relative to the actual compass directions, so that the zones aligned with the correct quarters of the real sky. Get the orientation wrong and every reading inverts. This is a skill with a correct and incorrect execution — which is exactly why a training model in durable bronze would be worth commissioning.

The books that Rome quoted and nobody kept

Behind the instrument stood a literature. The Romans called it the Etrusca disciplina, and described it as transmitted in three groups of books:

  • The libri haruspicini, on reading entrails.
  • The libri fulgurales, on lightning — its regions, its meanings, and the rituals for handling a struck place.
  • The libri rituales, on ritual law: how to found a city, lay out boundaries, consecrate temples, and how long the ages of the world would last.

That third category deserves emphasis. Roman city-founding ritual — the sacred boundary, the orientation, the plough drawn around the perimeter — was understood by Romans themselves to derive from Etruscan practice. This was not a fringe superstition Rome tolerated. It was structural.

Rome took it seriously enough to institutionalise it. There was an official order of haruspices. Etruscan noble families supplied practitioners, and young Romans were sent to learn the discipline. Haruspices were consulted by the Senate, attached to emperors, and still operating in the fourth century CE. In 408 CE, with Alaric outside the walls, haruspices are reported to have offered to call down lightning on the Gothic army.

Not one of those books survives. We possess quotations, summaries, and hostile paraphrases in Latin authors — Cicero, Seneca, Pliny, Macrobius, Ammianus — and nothing else.

The second lock on the door

Even the fragments that survive in Etruscan are only partly legible, and the reason is unusual.

The alphabet is not the problem. Etruscan was written in a script derived from Greek, and it can be transliterated with confidence — we know how the words sounded. The problem is the language itself. Etruscan is not Indo-European. It has no securely established relatives beyond the fragmentary Lemnian and Raetic, which are too poorly attested to help much.

So there is no cognate vocabulary to work from. Meaning has to be extracted from context, from bilingual inscriptions where they exist, and from repetition — and the surviving corpus of roughly thirteen thousand inscriptions is dominated by short funerary formulae. Names, ages, family relationships, offices. Enormously useful for grammar and titles; almost useless for theology.

The longest surviving Etruscan text reached us by a route that is difficult to improve on for strangeness. A linen book, apparently a ritual calendar of around twelve hundred readable words, was at some point cut into strips and used to wrap an Egyptian mummy. It was acquired in Egypt in the nineteenth century, taken to Croatia, and only later recognised for what it was. It survives because someone in Ptolemaic Egypt needed bandages.

Even so, it can only be partly understood. We can identify it as a calendar of rites, pick out gods and dates and repeated ritual instructions, and follow the structure — without reliably knowing what the ceremonies were for.

The dictionary that would have solved it

There is one loss here that is genuinely painful to contemplate.

The emperor Claudius was a serious scholar with an antiquarian obsession, and his first wife was Etruscan. He wrote a history of the Etruscans in twenty books — the Tyrrhenica — and is reported to have compiled a dictionary of the language. He had access to native speakers, to family archives, and to a priesthood still practising. He had the resources of the Roman state and no shortage of time.

Every word of it is gone.

No fire is required to explain this. Texts survive only where somebody keeps choosing to copy them, and a twenty-book history of a defunct people in a dead language, by an emperor with a poor posthumous reputation, was never going to compete for scribal labour against Virgil.

What we have instead is a piece of bronze from a ploughed field: forty compartments, forty divine names, and a discipline that shaped how Rome founded its cities and read its sky — reduced to a hand-sized object that we can hold, and describe, and not quite read.
