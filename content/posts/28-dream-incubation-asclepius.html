A woman called Ambrosia came to Epidaurus from Athens, blind in one eye. Walking round the sanctuary before her night in the hall, she read the accounts of cures carved on the stones and thought they were ridiculous — the lame walking, the blind seeing on the strength of a dream — and said so.

That night the god appeared to her. He told her he would cure the eye. He also told her that in payment she was to dedicate a silver pig in the sanctuary, as a memorial of her stupidity.

She woke up able to see, and paid.

What makes that story worth starting with isn't the cure. It's that the sanctuary carved it. Somebody at Epidaurus decided the official record should include a sceptic, by name, with her objection stated clearly — and then the god's reply.

What actually happened to you

The sequence was standardised and public.

You arrived, and you were purified — washing, and restrictions on what you'd eaten and done. You sacrificed. You made an offering, scaled to what you could afford; the sanctuary was not only for the rich, and the inscriptions include slaves.

Then, at night, you were taken into the abaton — a long open-sided hall, its name meaning roughly "the place not to be walked into", because access was controlled. You lay down on a pallet among other patients. The lamps were put out.

And you waited for the god.

In the morning, you reported what you had dreamt. If the god had healed you outright, you were healed. If he had prescribed something — a diet, an exercise, a bath, a place to go — the staff helped interpret it and you did as instructed.

Then you paid your thank-offering, and if you wanted, you left a tablet describing what had happened.

Seventy accounts, carved and curated

Those tablets are the origin of the strangest medical archive to survive from antiquity.

In the fourth century BCE the sanctuary gathered up the individual dedications and had them compiled onto large stone stelae — the iamata, the cures. Pausanias saw six of them standing when he visited some five hundred years later. Four survive, two of them substantially, and between them they preserve around seventy accounts.

The format barely varies. A name. Where they were from. The complaint. What the god did while they slept. The result.

And they're genuinely peculiar reading, because the medicine and the impossible sit side by side without any change of tone.

Some are the sort of thing that happens. Abscesses that drain. Kidney stones passed. Ulcers that close. Paralysis that lifts. A man with a spear point lodged in his jaw for six years, which the god removes in the night and hands to him — and he walks out holding it.

Others are not. A woman pregnant for five years, who gives birth to a boy that washes himself in a spring and walks away. A man whose head has been severed and reattached. Worms removed from a belly by opening it up and sewing it shut again.

The compiler treated both kinds as the same kind of thing, which tells you the collection was never trying to be a plausible record. It was trying to demonstrate range.

The stones were part of the treatment

Here is the detail that reorganises everything, and it's the one Ambrosia's story hands you.

The stelae stood where suppliants would see them before they slept.

So the last thing a patient did before lying down in the dark, in an unfamiliar building, after fasting and ritual washing and sacrifice, in a state of considerable hope, was read seventy detailed descriptions of what this god does to people at night: how he appears, what he says, that he sometimes cuts, that he sometimes sends a snake or a dog to lick the wound, that he expects payment and punishes those who don't.

You went to sleep having been comprehensively briefed on the dream you were about to have.

Lynn LiDonnici's work on the collection makes the editorial hand visible — these are not raw patient statements but a compilation, shaped, grouped and rewritten to a house style. Read that way, the stelae are simultaneously an archive, an advertisement, and a script.

Which is not a debunking. It's a description of how the thing worked. A sanctuary that produced results was a sanctuary that taught its patients precisely what to expect, and the teaching was carved in stone at the entrance.

Did any of it work?

Partly, probably, and we can't take it apart.

Several unremarkable mechanisms were available. Sick people arrived at a clean site with good water, rested, ate a regulated diet, and stopped working — which alone will improve a range of conditions. Expectation is a documented and powerful thing, particularly for pain and for symptoms with a functional component. Some conditions resolve on their own, and the person who recovers at the sanctuary is the one who leaves a tablet.

There's a more uncomfortable possibility too, which the surgical accounts raise: that in some cases something was physically done to a sedated or sleeping patient, and reported afterwards as the god's work. The evidence doesn't settle it either way.

And the failures are invisible. Nobody dedicated a tablet saying the god ignored them. The one long first-person account we have — the Sacred Tales of Aelius Aristides, an orator who spent years taking dream instructions from Asclepius, including bathing in rivers in midwinter — is priceless and wildly unrepresentative, because Aristides was a man who never got better and never stopped.

Nobody thought this was the alternative

The last thing worth clearing up is the assumption that this was Greek medicine's rival.

It wasn't. Hippocratic physicians and the Asclepius sanctuaries operated at the same time, in the same cities, for centuries. Doctors dedicated at Asclepieia. The god was, after all, the patron of their profession.

An ancient patient with a bad eye might visit a physician, wear an engraved stone cut for exactly that complaint, and sleep in the abaton — and would have found the question of which one "really" worked slightly beside the point. You applied everything available and hoped.

Ambrosia went home with her sight and, if she kept her word, left behind a small silver pig with her name on it: a piece of sanctuary property recording that a visitor had doubted the advertising, and been billed for it.
