Before a battle, a Roman commander had a job to do that had nothing to do with troop positions. He had to feed some chickens and watch them eat.

Not casually. There was an office for it: the pullarii, chicken-keepers, whose entire function was maintaining a cage of birds and reporting how they behaved with food in front of them. If the chickens ate with appetite, especially if a scrap fell from a beak to the ground, the gods approved. If they picked at it, or refused outright, the gods did not, and no responsible commander proceeded.

In 249 BCE, off the coast of Sicily, a consul found out what happened when you called the gods' bluff.

The morning before Drepana

Publius Claudius Pulcher commanded the Roman fleet in the closing years of the First Punic War, and he wanted to hit the Carthaginian fleet at Drepana before it could regroup. On the morning of the attack, the pullarii brought out the sacred chickens as protocol required. The chickens would not eat.

According to Cicero, Valerius Maximus and Suetonius, all writing well over a century after the event, Pulcher's response was to have the birds thrown overboard, with a line that got repeated for the rest of Roman history: since they didn't want to eat, let them drink.

He fought the battle anyway. It went about as badly as a Roman naval defeat could go. Most sources put the losses in the dozens of ships, a rout so severe that Pulcher was recalled to Rome, prosecuted, and fined. Roman tradition remembered the fine as being specifically for the impiety, not just the defeat, and later writers folded in a story that he died soon after, divine punishment arriving on schedule.

What augury actually was

It is easy to hear this story and file Roman religion under superstition. That undersells what augury was built to do.

Augury was not fortune-telling. It was a formal system for checking whether a specific proposed action, a battle, an election, a piece of legislation, had divine clearance to proceed, on that day, in that place. The college of augurs that staffed it in Rome was a distinct institution from the Etruscan-trained haruspices who read lightning strikes and animal entrails. Augury was native Roman practice, and its officers were senior political figures, not hired technicians.

Signs came in two flavours. There were auspicia oblativa, unsolicited signs that simply happened, a bird crossing left to right, thunder at the wrong moment, and auspicia impetrativa, signs deliberately requested, like the chicken-feeding before Drepana. The whole apparatus assumed the gods were communicating specifically, on request, about specific human plans. That is a bigger theological claim than reading a horoscope. It is closer to a standing hotline.

The tool this became

The part of the system that made augury genuinely powerful in daily political life was not the battlefield drama. It was obnuntiatio, the right to halt public business by announcing an unfavourable omen.

Any magistrate who held the augural power could, in principle, stop an assembly already in session by declaring the sky had turned against it. Cicero's own letters record political opponents using exactly this move to block legislation they could not defeat by argument. A senator who lost a vote fair and square had, if he happened to also be an augur, a second route: claim the gods objected.

Which raises an obvious question about a man like Cicero himself.

The augur who didn't believe the birds

Cicero was an augur. He was also, in his philosophical work, one of Roman antiquity's sharpest critics of divination as a genuine predictive science. In On Divination he takes the Drepana story and other omens apart methodically, arguing that chance alone guarantees enough coincidental hits to sustain a superstition indefinitely, and that a bird's appetite tells you nothing whatsoever about naval tactics.

Then, in the same work, he argues for keeping the whole system running.

Not because it works. Because a state needs shared rituals of authority, and augury supplied one that was old, familiar and stabilising, whatever its predictive value. Cicero's position, in effect, is that the sacred chickens are nonsense and the college that consults them should be preserved regardless, for the sake of public order rather than public truth. The modern historian Federico Santangelo, examining Republican divination as a political rather than purely religious institution, reads this as entirely characteristic of how the Roman elite actually used these systems: less a belief to be interrogated than a lever to be operated, by people who mostly knew which hand was on it.

What Pulcher actually got wrong

Seen this way, Pulcher's mistake at Drepana was not impiety in some simple, sincere sense. It was doing the quiet part out loud.

Ignoring an inconvenient omen was not unheard of in Roman practice; commanders found workarounds for unfavourable signs regularly enough that the sources record the manoeuvres by name. What Pulcher did instead was mock the mechanism itself, in front of witnesses, and then lose anyway. Rome could tolerate a general who quietly sidestepped the chickens. A general who threw them in the sea and lost a fleet had made the whole apparatus look exactly as arbitrary as Cicero privately thought it was, and that was the unforgivable part.

The chickens were never really about chickens. They were an instrument for asking whether a plan had public backing loud enough to survive contact with bad luck. Pulcher found out what happens when the instrument gets thrown overboard along with the birds.
