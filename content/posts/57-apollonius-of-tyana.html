Ephesus had the plague, and the city sent for Apollonius.

He gathered the people at the theatre and pointed to an old beggar in rags, a crust of bread in his bag, his eyes screwed half shut. Stone him, Apollonius said. The Ephesians balked. The old man was a stranger, and he was pleading with them. Then he looked up, and his eyes were full of fire.

They buried him under a cairn. When Apollonius had the stones cleared, there was no beggar. Underneath lay a huge dog the size of a lion, crushed, foaming at the mouth like a rabid one. A statue of Heracles the Averter was put up on the spot.

That's book 4, chapter 10 of Philostratus' Life of Apollonius of Tyana. It's a fair sample of the book: vivid, precise about the props, and almost impossible to check.

One book, written late

Apollonius came from Tyana in Cappadocia, in what's now central Turkey, and lived through most of the first century CE. He was a Pythagorean of the strict kind: long hair, no meat, no wine, linen instead of wool, and five years without speaking. He travelled. Philostratus sends him to Babylon, to the Brahmans of India, to the Naked Sages of Ethiopia, to Rome and Spain and back.

Nearly all of that rests on Philostratus, an Athenian sophist writing in the early third century, well over a hundred years after Apollonius died. He says the empress Julia Domna, wife of Septimius Severus, gave him the memoirs of Damis of Nineveh, a disciple who went everywhere with the master, and asked him to make them into literature. She died in 217, before he finished.

Nobody else ever saw a line of Damis. Ewen Bowie's long study of 1978 concluded the Damis material is Philostratus' own work, and most scholars now agree he was invented, or that "his" memoir was a forgery Philostratus took on trust. Maria Dzielska went through the biography in 1986 looking for anything she could anchor outside it, and found very little.

Philostratus does name a rival: an earlier biography by one Moeragenes, which he dismisses as ill-informed. It doesn't survive. We'll come back to what it said.

What survives outside the book

Take Philostratus away and something is still left, though not much.

Cassius Dio, a senator writing in the same years, tells one story independently. On the day Domitian was murdered in Rome in 96 CE, Apollonius was at Ephesus, or somewhere else, and climbed onto a rock and shouted to the crowd: "Good, Stephanus! Bravo, Stephanus! Strike the bloodthirsty wretch!" Stephanus was the assassin. Philostratus has the scene too, with different details, so Dio probably didn't take it from him.

Dio also says Caracalla, Julia Domna's son, built Apollonius a shrine. A verse inscription now in the museum at Adana, in southern Turkey, praises a man "named for Apollo" who "shone forth from Tyana" and put out the faults of men. Its broken lines have been restored in more than one way, and Christopher Jones read them as saying the sage still helps mankind from heaven. Whatever the exact wording, someone paid to have him honoured in stone.

And then there's the reputation. Lucian, writing around 180, sneers that the fraudulent prophet Alexander of Abonoteichus was trained by a former follower of Apollonius who knew all his stagecraft. Origen, a Christian writing around 248, quotes Moeragenes: Apollonius was a magician and a philosopher, and some philosophers fell under the spell of his magic.

So before Philostratus wrote a word, the first thing people said about Apollonius was that he was a magus.

The vanishing defendant

That is the charge Philostratus was writing against. His Apollonius is a sage whose powers come from purity and wisdom, and the book keeps saying so, sometimes defensively. A goês, the Greek word for a wizard, was a paid trickster who forced the gods with spells, and whose trade had old links to calling up the dead. Apollonius, we're told, never did anything of the kind.

The climax is a trial. Domitian has Apollonius arrested in Rome, cuts off his hair and puts him in chains. The questions put to him in court are a list of what a magician would be accused of: why he dresses differently, why people call him a god, how he foretold the plague at Ephesus, and whether he went into the countryside and cut up a boy as a sacrifice for Domitian's rival Nerva. It's the same kind of case Apuleius faced in his own magic trial in North Africa, where accusers read sinister meaning into ordinary habits.

Domitian acquits him. Apollonius makes a short speech about the informers ruining the empire, quotes Homer, "for you shall not kill me, since I am not mortal", and vanishes from the courtroom. That evening he turns up at Dicaearchia, on the Bay of Naples, where Damis takes him for a ghost.

Philostratus then prints the long defence speech Apollonius had prepared and never needed. You can see the author's problem. To prove his hero was no sorcerer, he has him do the single most sorcerous thing in the book.

The rival

Around 303 CE, Sossianus Hierocles, a provincial governor and an active persecutor of Christians, wrote a treatise called The Lover of Truth setting Apollonius' miracles against those of Jesus. Look, he said: a wise man did all this, and nobody calls him a god.

A reply survives under the name of Eusebius of Caesarea, though whether Eusebius wrote it has been doubted since 1992. It grants that Apollonius may have been a decent philosopher, and assigns the miracles to demons. That settled his reputation among Christians for over a thousand years. The Historia Augusta claims the emperor Severus Alexander kept Apollonius in his private shrine beside Christ, Abraham and Orpheus. That collection invents things for fun, so the claim tells us more about how the pairing looked by the late fourth century than about any emperor.

The Life itself never mentions Christians. Whether Philostratus had Jesus in mind is one of the questions people keep asking and nobody can answer.

The sage as hardware

The strangest afterlife came later. By the sixth century, the chronicler John Malalas and the stories collected about Constantinople's monuments credited Apollonius with telesmata, talismans planted in cities to keep harm out. In Antioch they guarded against scorpions and the north wind. In Byzantium, against storks, and floods on the river Lykos.

None of this is in Philostratus. The philosopher who wouldn't wear wool had become a sort of municipal pest control, and the objects were still being pointed out to visitors centuries after his death.
