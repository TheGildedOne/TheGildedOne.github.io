A woman arriving at the festival could not wear transparent fabric. She could not wear gold. She could not turn up with her hair bound up under a band, or her face painted white with lead, or her cheeks reddened with the rouge made from seaweed. There were limits on what her clothes were allowed to cost.

If she wore something forbidden, an official could confiscate it on the spot, and the garment became the property of the gods. She did not get it back.

All of that is cut into a single block of stone from Messenia, across 194 lines, along with the fines, the seating, the water supply and the name of the man who handed over the keys.

What it never mentions, anywhere, is what the mystery actually was.

The most regulated secret in Greece

The Andania inscription is the fullest rulebook any Greek mystery cult left behind, with the single exception of Eleusis. It is thorough to the point of comedy.

It appoints the officials and says how they are chosen. It sets the order of the procession. It rules on the tents people pitched at the sanctuary and how big they were allowed to be. It covers the fountain, the money, the audit of the money, and what happens to an official who fails that audit.

It legislates for slaves and for who may be freed at the sanctuary. It fixes penalties in cash. And it records that the sacred books and the ritual implements were handed over by a man named Mnasistratos, a short phrase carrying a large event: a cult passing out of private hands into the keeping of a city.

On the rite itself, the thing all of this exists to protect, it says nothing whatsoever.

The silence is not an accident

It would be easy to read that as frustrating, or as proof the secret was well kept. It is neither, quite.

Greek sacred laws are administrative documents. They exist to settle disputes, allocate money and tell people where to stand. Nobody carved 194 lines of public stone to explain theology, any more than a fire regulation explains what a theatre is for.

So the silence tells us about the genre before it tells us about the secret. What it does prove is that the two were separable. You could publish the entire apparatus of a mystery cult in the open, on a stone anyone could walk up and read, and the mystery survived it intact. The rite was not the paperwork.

That is worth holding onto, because it is the mistake the evidence keeps tempting us into. At Eleusis we have the building, the calendar, the fees and the procession route, and not the thing seen in the hall. The pattern repeats because what survives is always administrative. Buildings and budgets last. Experiences do not. At Samothrace it is worse still, and we cannot even reliably name the gods being worshipped.

Why the dress code is the interesting part

Go back to the clothes, because a rule is a fossil of a behaviour.

You do not write a law banning gold and expensive fabric at a religious festival unless people have been turning up in gold and expensive fabric. The dress code is evidence of a real social problem. Initiation in these cults was generally open to anyone who could pay and turn up, so the sanctuary drew a mixed crowd, and some of that crowd treated a sacred procession as a chance to be looked at.

The cosmetics clause is sharper still. The law has specific vocabulary for what women put on their faces, including a white lead preparation and a seaweed rouge. The drafters were not gesturing vaguely at modesty. They knew the products by name.

Read that way the stone stops being a rulebook and starts being a complaint. Somebody at Andania was extremely annoyed.

The enforcement has the same flavour. An official could simply take the offending item, and it became sanctuary property. That is a rule written by people who had already tried asking nicely.

The problem nobody has solved

Here is the genuinely unresolved thing, and it matters more than it sounds.

The inscription dates itself to year 55. The question is: fifty-five years after what.

The traditional reading takes it as the Achaean era, which puts the stone at 91 or 92 BCE, in the late Hellenistic world, a generation after Rome began rearranging Greece. On that reading Andania is a Greek city reorganising a Greek cult under its own steam.

Petros Themelis argued for the Actian era instead, counted from Actium. That reading moves the whole document to 24 CE, into the Roman empire proper, under Tiberius. The same stone becomes a provincial community reviving an old rite inside somebody else's world, which is a completely different story about why anyone bothered.

Laura Gawlinski went through both cases in her edition and came down cautiously against the redating, saying she was not fully persuaded. The majority position holds. It holds without much comfort.

A hundred and fifteen years is a long time to be uncertain about a document this precise. It is the difference between a Hellenistic revival and a Roman one, and every conclusion anyone wants to draw about why the mysteries were reorganised depends on which it is.

The man who handed it over

Which leaves Mnasistratos, the one human being in all this, and the one we cannot reach.

He appears as the person who gave up the sacred books and the implements. He is honoured for it and keeps privileges. The text, being administration, does not spend a syllable on why.

Every reconstruction is a guess. Perhaps a family had held the rite privately and could no longer afford it. Perhaps the city wanted control and he was the graceful way to take it. Perhaps he was old, and there was nobody after him.

What survives is the receipt. A man handed over the books, the city took charge, the rules went into stone, and a woman in the wrong hairband could have it taken off her at the gate.

The books themselves, the only object here that would have told us what any of it meant, are gone without a trace.
