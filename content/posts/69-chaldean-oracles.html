The legion was surrounded, it was summer, and there was no water. The Quadi had the Romans penned in and were happy to wait for thirst to finish the job.

Then the sky went dark and the rain came down so hard that the soldiers stood with their faces turned up, mouths open, catching it in their shields and helmets. Hail and lightning fell on the enemy instead.

That's Cassius Dio's account of a day in Marcus Aurelius' war on the Danube, in the early 170s CE. The emperor's column in Rome shows it too: a huge, gloomy rain god with his arms and wings spread over the army, water pouring off him in sheets.

What nobody could agree on was who had made it happen.

Too many rainmakers

Dio gives the credit to Arnouphis, an Egyptian magician travelling with the emperor, who called on Hermes. Christian writers said the storm answered the prayers of Christian soldiers in the legion. Other sources say Marcus prayed for it himself. And a Byzantine encyclopedia, the Suda, gives it to a man named Julian, a theurgist, who made dark clouds gather over the army. Then it adds, honestly enough, that others say it was Arnouphis.

The Suda is also nearly all we know about Julian. It says his father, another Julian, was a "Chaldean" philosopher, and that the son wrote books on theurgy and ritual, and oracles in epic verse. Those oracles are the subject here.

So the evidence that the poem's author was a rainmaker comes from a dictionary compiled in the tenth century, eight hundred years after the storm, which wasn't sure about the rain either.

Scripture in pieces

The poem doesn't survive. What we have are about 226 fragments, a line here and three lines there, quoted by philosophers who took them as the word of the gods.

They took them very seriously. Porphyry, Iamblichus and Proclus all wrote commentaries on the Oracles, and every one of those is lost. Proclus, who ran the Platonic school at Athens in the fifth century, told his students that if it were up to him, he'd let every ancient book disappear except two: the Oracles and Plato's Timaeus. Franz Cumont later called the Oracles the Bible of the Neoplatonists, and the name stuck.

The longest commentary that survives is by Michael Psellus, a Christian courtier in eleventh-century Constantinople, who found the poem fascinating and plainly didn't trust it. Some of what passes for the text online is arranged from his summaries, which scholars don't accept as the poem's real order.

The theology, pieced together, goes like this. At the top is the Father, so far beyond the world that thought can't reach him. Below him an Intellect fashioned the cosmos. Between the two stands Hecate, a sort of membrane between the Father's pure fire and the material world, through which every divine power passes on its way down. Souls fell into bodies, and with the right help they can climb back.

None of that is Babylonian. It's Greek Platonism of the second century, the same ideas you'd find in philosophers of the day like Numenius. John Dillon has pointed out that the gods who spoke to the Julians held remarkably up-to-date views on Plato. "Chaldean" was probably a label for exotic wisdom, the way "Egyptian" was on the spells in the Greek magical papyri.

Doing things to the gods

What made the Oracles different was not the theology. It was the claim that you could do something about it.

That something was theurgy, "god-work". The word itself first turns up in the Oracles, which promise that theurgists do not fall in with the herd that Fate drives. The fragments tell the initiate to keep silent, to purify himself, to work the spinning instrument called the strophalos of Hecate. They describe what he might see: a formless fire that speaks, a flash of light, a child on horseback, made of fire or gold, shooting arrows.

It was the same century in which Apollo at Claros was being asked what god is, and Alexander of Abonoteichus was selling oracles from a snake with a human-looking head. Gods were talking a great deal in the second century.

Iamblichus, defending theurgy a century later, made the boldest claim for it. Thinking doesn't join the theurgist to the gods, he wrote. The rites do, performed correctly, even when the man performing them doesn't understand them. Augustine, attacking Porphyry in the City of God, thought this was exactly the problem: philosophers who should have known better, dabbling in what he called demon-worship.

Magic in a philosopher's cloak?

Modern scholars split along roughly the same line. E. R. Dodds, in 1947, read Iamblichus' defence as a surrender of reason: salvation by ritual rather than by thought, the end of Greek philosophy as philosophy. Hans Lewy, whose huge study appeared after his death in 1956, tried to reconstruct the Chaldean system as a whole, rites and all,. Sarah Iles Johnston traced what the Oracles did with Hecate, turning a goddess of crossroads and ghosts into the soul of the cosmos. Gregory Shaw has argued that Iamblichus was a serious philosopher with good reasons for putting ritual first.

Underneath the argument is a plain gap. We don't have one complete theurgic rite, from beginning to end. We don't know whether the Oracles were one poem or a collection, whether the Julians composed them or took them down from someone in a trance, or whether the two Julians were quite the people the Suda describes.

The column in Rome doesn't name anyone either. It shows the soldiers, the storm, and the god, with water running off his wings and beard.
