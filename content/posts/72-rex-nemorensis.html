He walks the edge of the grove with a drawn sword, looking over his shoulder. He's a runaway slave. He got the job by killing the man who had it, and he'll keep it until somebody kills him.

That's Strabo, writing under Augustus about the sanctuary of Diana near Aricia, on the shore of the little crater lake we now call Nemi, thirty kilometres south-east of Rome. He calls the custom "Scythian", which is Greek for barbarous, and goes back to describing the lake.

Eighteen centuries later a Cambridge don opened a book with the same man. "Who does not know Turner's picture of the Golden Bough?" asked James George Frazer in 1890, and then spent two volumes, eventually twelve, explaining the priest of Nemi by way of almost every custom on earth.

Four witnesses and a footnote

The Romans themselves said very little, and it doesn't fit together as neatly as Frazer needed.

Strabo gives the runaway, the killing and the sword. Pausanias, in the second century CE, adds that the priesthood was a prize for single combat, open to no free man, only to slaves who had run from their masters. Oddly, he mentions it while describing Epidaurus, where an old slab recorded that Hippolytus had dedicated twenty horses to Asclepius, the god who healed people in their sleep. The Aricians, he says, claimed Asclepius had raised Hippolytus from the dead, and that he came to Italy and became their king.

Ovid gives one couplet: the kings there rule by strong hands and quick feet, and each dies by the precedent he set. Suetonius supplies the title rex Nemorensis, "king of the grove", in a list of Caligula's spiteful acts. The emperor, irritated that the current king had lasted many years, hired a stronger challenger to take him on. Suetonius doesn't say who won.

And the bough? It isn't in any of them. It comes from Servius, a grammarian of about 400 CE, in his note on the passage where the Sibyl of Cumae tells Aeneas he'll need a golden branch to enter the underworld. In the grove at Aricia, Servius says, stood a tree from which nobody was allowed to break a branch. A runaway slave who managed it won the right to fight the priest.

So the detail that gave Frazer his title comes from one commentator, writing four centuries after Strabo, as a gloss on a poem. Servius is the first writer to put Virgil's branch and the Arician tree side by side. Nobody earlier does.

Frazer's tree

Frazer asked two questions. Why must the priest kill his predecessor? And why the branch?

His answer took him round the world. The priest, he argued, was a divine king, the living embodiment of the spirit of the tree, and the health of nature depended on his strength. When that began to fail he had to be killed and replaced by a stronger man, so that the god's life passed on undiminished. The branch was mistletoe, the plant that killed the Norse god Balder, and the priest's life was bound up in it.

It's a tremendous argument, and very little of it is about Nemi. In 1973 Jonathan Z. Smith published an essay called "When the Bough Breaks" that went through Frazer's chain of reasoning link by link and found it failing at nearly every one. Nothing in the ancient evidence says the priest was divine, that he was killed when he weakened, or that the tree was an oak with mistletoe on it.

What the grove was for

The sanctuary was dug in the 1880s, badly. Lord Savile, the British ambassador, got a licence in 1885. The landowner, Prince Orsini, kept the best pieces and sold them, and Savile gave his share to the Castle Museum in Nottingham, which is why some of Diana's offerings ended up in the English Midlands.

What came out of the ground doesn't look like a cult of doomed kings. It looks like a busy healing shrine: terracotta models of the body parts people wanted mended, and offerings asking for children and an easy birth. C. M. C. Green, whose 2007 study is the fullest modern account, describes the goddess as family doctor, psychiatrist and vet in one. Ovid says the grove was hung with votive tablets. Statius pictures the lake on the Ides of August, Diana's day, glittering with worshippers' torches.

It was also political. Cato copied a dedication of the grove by Egerius Baebius of Tusculum, "Latin dictator", on behalf of a list of Latin towns. Like Fortuna's oracle at Praeneste a few hills away, this was a Latin sanctuary, not a Roman one.

Green argues that the king makes sense without Frazer. The cult's founding stories are about fugitives: Orestes fleeing the Furies with Diana's image, Hippolytus fleeing death itself. A runaway slave as priest fits that pattern, and so does a king who rules exactly as long as he can defend himself. To her the rite belongs inside Latin ideas about kingship and exile, not a universal theory of tree spirits. Not every reviewer was persuaded, partly because her case still leans heavily on Servius.

What we don't know

Most of it. We don't know how often a challenger turned up, or whether by the first century the duel was a real fight or a show. We don't know what the king did on an ordinary day besides watch the trees. Strabo, Ovid, Pausanias and Suetonius all treat him as a curiosity, and not one of them says he went to look.

Caligula, the emperor who sent the stronger man, also kept two enormous ships on the lake. They were raised when Mussolini drained it between 1928 and 1932, put on show in a museum on the shore, and burned there in 1944.
