Four days into the desert, the water ran out.

Alexander had left the coast at Paraetonium in the winter of 332–331 BCE and turned south, into sand, towards the oasis where the god Ammon gave oracles. Then a wind came up and buried the track, and the guides lost their way.

What happened next depends on whom you read. It rained, the sources agree, out of a clear desert sky. Then something led them to the oasis. Callisthenes, the expedition's official historian, said two crows flew ahead, and called back stragglers by cawing. Aristobulus, an engineer on the campaign, also said two birds. Ptolemy, who later ruled Egypt and wrote his own history, said two snakes, and that they talked.

Arrian, writing four centuries later with all three in front of him, decides that some divine help was given, and that the disagreements have robbed the story of certainty. He's being polite. That's the pattern for everything that happened at Siwa.

An oracle Greeks already trusted

Ammon was the Egyptian Amun, a god with the horns of a ram, and Greeks had treated his oasis shrine as one of their own oracles for a long time. Herodotus says Croesus of Lydia tested it in the sixth century BCE, along with Delphi and Dodona. Herodotus also tells how two black doves flew from Egyptian Thebes, one to found the oracle at Dodona and the other the oracle of Ammon in Libya. Pindar wrote him a hymn. The Athenians named one of their sacred state ships after him.

The temple still stands on the rock of Aghurmi in the middle of the oasis, mud and stone, usually dated to the pharaoh Amasis. It's small. What it lacked in size it made up in method.

Diodorus and Curtius describe the god's image: an object shaped, Curtius says, rather like a navel, set with emeralds and other gems. When someone asked a question, priests carried it on their shoulders in a gilded boat, eighty of them according to Diodorus, with women singing behind. The god answered by moving them. A lurch this way or that, and the prophet read it out.

What he heard

Alexander went in alone. Callisthenes says the priest let him enter in his ordinary clothes, while everyone else changed and heard the responses from outside. Callisthenes also says the answers came mostly by nods and signs, not words. Except one: the priest told Alexander, in so many words, that he was the son of Zeus.

Diodorus and Curtius give a fuller dialogue. The old prophet greets him as "son", and says the title comes from the god. Alexander asks whether he will rule the whole earth. The boat moves; yes. He asks whether all his father's murderers have been punished. The prophet replies that no mortal could harm the one who fathered him; as for Philip's killers, they had all paid. Curtius, who writes in Latin under the empire, can't resist a dig: the priest, he says, was composed for flattery.

Arrian, the most careful of them, gives none of it. Alexander heard, Arrian says, what was agreeable to him, "as he himself said". Then he went back to Egypt.

That's the real problem. Nobody but Alexander and the priests was in the room. Callisthenes wasn't. Every version of the dialogue is either something Alexander chose to say, or something his historians decided he must have heard. And Callisthenes was writing for a king who wanted to be the son of a god. This is the same Callisthenes who reported that the oracle at Didyma, silent for generations, had found its sacred spring flowing again just as Alexander arrived in Asia.

A slip of the tongue

Plutarch has the best story, and he's careful to say only that some people tell it.

The priest, wanting to be courteous, tried to greet Alexander in Greek as O paidion, "my child". His accent let him down, and he ended the word with an s instead of an n. It came out as O pai Dios: "son of Zeus". Alexander was delighted by the mistake, and the story went round that the god had called him his son.

It's a lovely pun, and it's in no other source. It reads like the work of someone who wanted to keep the famous greeting and explain it away at the same time.

There's a duller explanation, and it may be the right one. To an Egyptian priest, the ruler of Egypt was the son of Amun as a matter of protocol. It was part of the pharaoh's titles. Francisco Bosch-Puche has gathered the Egyptian titles Alexander received from Egypt's priesthood, which call him beloved of Amun and chosen of Ra, and a few inscriptions go further and call him son of Amun. If that's what the priest said, he was being correct, not prophetic. Alexander's Greeks heard a revelation.

What he did with it

Whatever he heard, he didn't let it go. Arrian says that at the mouth of the Indus, years later, he sacrificed to the gods Ammon had named for him. When his friend Hephaestion died in 324 BCE, Alexander sent envoys back across the desert to Siwa to ask how he should be honoured. The answer came back: as a hero, not a god.

Ernst Fredricksmeyer argued that Alexander took his bond with Ammon seriously all the way through the conquest of Asia. How literally he believed it is still argued, from the same handful of texts. Callisthenes himself, the man who reported the sonship, later refused to bow down to Alexander as if to a god. He was arrested in 327 BCE and died in custody.

Plutarch preserves one more detail, from a letter he says Alexander wrote to his mother, Olympias. He had received certain secret answers at Siwa, he told her, which he would tell to her alone when he got home.

He never got home. He died at Babylon in 323 BCE, and she never saw him again.
