In a museum in Berlin there's a circular painted wooden panel about thirty centimetres across, made in Egypt around 200 CE. It shows an imperial family: Septimius Severus with a grey beard, his wife Julia Domna, and their two sons.

One of the sons has no face. The paint has been scraped and smeared away, leaving a rough brown void where a boy's head used to be.

His name was Geta. In 211 CE his brother Caracalla had him killed — by most accounts in their mother's arms — and then set about removing him from the world. Someone in Egypt who owned this small domestic portrait understood what was now expected, and took something abrasive to the face of a child they had presumably hung on a wall.

That panel is the clearest surviving image of what modern historians call damnatio memoriae.

What it involved

Not one act but a bundle, applied in varying combinations by decree of the Senate, usually after the target was already dead.

  • Names chiselled from inscriptions — monuments, dedications, milestones, building plaques, anywhere it appeared in stone.
  • Images destroyed or recarved. Statues pulled down; marble portraits frequently reworked into a successor's likeness. Bronze went to the furnace.
  • Coinage withdrawn and melted, though hoard evidence shows this was never comprehensive.
  • Records struck — removed from the consular lists, from acts, from the calendar.
  • The name itself forbidden. In severe cases relatives were barred from using it, and the birthday declared inauspicious.

Suetonius describes the aftermath of Domitian's assassination in 96 CE: senators arriving with ladders, tearing his portraits down on the spot and smashing them, voting to erase his inscriptions everywhere. Pliny the Younger, who was there, describes the same scene with undisguised pleasure — axes going into the faces, the imperial features struck off.

Worth noting that damnatio memoriae isn't a phrase Romans used. It's modern, from early legal scholarship, and it imposes a tidy unified concept on what was in practice an ad hoc and highly variable set of penalties. Some targets got everything. Some got a few statues pulled down. Some were condemned and quietly rehabilitated later.

Geta, and the machinery at full power

Caracalla's erasure of his brother is the case where everything ran at once, and it's well documented precisely because it failed so visibly.

On the Arch of the Argentarii in Rome — a small monument put up by the money-changers' guild — the family is shown sacrificing. Geta's figure has been cut out of the relief, leaving an obvious rectangular hole in the composition with the surviving figures gesturing at nothing. The dedicatory inscription has lines hacked away.

Papyri from Egypt show his name blotted out of official documents. Inscriptions across the empire carry the gap. Cassius Dio claims men were killed for owning his image or mourning him.

The result, eighteen centuries later, is that Geta is one of the better-known short-reigned emperors, largely because of the erasure. We can list where his name was removed, which monuments carry the scars, and roughly how fast the order propagated across the provinces. The deletion has a paper trail.

The structural problem

An erased inscription isn't a blank inscription. It's an inscription with a conspicuous scar in it, cut into a legible sentence, in a public place, surrounded by intact text explaining the context. Anyone literate walking past knows immediately that somebody has been removed, and can usually work out who from the remaining words.

Recarved statues are the same. Sculptors reworking a portrait left traces — proportions that no longer fit, hair recut over a different original, drill work at odd depths. Specialists identify reworked heads routinely.

Which is why the current consensus, argued most fully by Harriet Flower, has largely abandoned the idea that these were literal attempts at oblivion. The purpose was performance. The empty space was meant to be seen.

Read that way it becomes intelligible as politics. The Senate, or the successor, is making a public statement: this person's honours were illegitimate, this regime is repudiated, and the people doing the chiselling have picked a side. A dishonoured absence is a stronger message than a name nobody notices. The gap is the announcement.

The Egyptian versions, which aren't the same

Hatshepsut, who ruled as pharaoh in the fifteenth century BCE, had her images defaced and her name removed from monuments and king lists. The obvious reading — a resentful successor lashing out — hits a problem: the erasures seem to have begun about two decades after her death, and were selective rather than total. Immediate personal spite doesn't fit a twenty-year delay. Current interpretations lean toward dynastic housekeeping: tidying an anomaly out of the official line, without particular animus.

Akhenaten got harsher treatment. After his religious revolution collapsed, his city was abandoned, his monuments dismantled, and his name omitted from later king lists, which skip from his predecessor to Horemheb as though the intervening decades never happened. His temple blocks were reused as rubble fill inside later structures.

Which is why thousands of them survive in excellent condition — protected from weather for three thousand years by the very people trying to eliminate them. Egyptologists recovered the Amarna period substantially from that fill. The erasure preserved the evidence.

The uncomfortable part

Everything above is a failure, because failures are what leave traces.

We know about Geta because the erasure was clumsy and applied across an empire too large to police. We know about Akhenaten because his stones got reused rather than ground down. Every case here is a case where the procedure didn't work.

There's no way to identify the ones where it did. A successful erasure produces no gap to notice, no reworked portrait to detect, no anomaly in the record — because a complete removal also removes the evidence that anything was removed.

What we can say is that the ancient world had both the administrative capacity and the political will to try this, repeatedly, over millennia. Augustus burned two thousand books of unauthorised prophecy in a single operation, and we know about it from one passing line in Suetonius.

The blank spaces we can see are the ones where somebody was in a hurry.
