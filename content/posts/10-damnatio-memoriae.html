In the Antikensammlung in Berlin there is a circular painted wooden panel about thirty centimetres across, made in Egypt around 200 CE. It shows an imperial family: Septimius Severus with a grey beard, his wife Julia Domna, and their two sons.

One of the sons has no face. The paint has been scraped and smeared away, leaving a rough brown void where a boy's head was.

His name was Geta. In 211 CE his brother Caracalla had him killed — by most accounts in their mother's arms — and then set about removing him from the world. Someone in Egypt who owned this small domestic portrait understood what was now required of them, and took something abrasive to the face of a child they had presumably hung on a wall.

That panel is the clearest surviving image of what modern historians call damnatio memoriae: the Roman procedure for deleting a person.

What the sanction involved

It was not a single act but a bundle of measures, applied in varying combinations by decree of the Senate, usually after the target was already dead.

  • Names chiselled from inscriptions. Public monuments, dedications, milestones, building plaques — anywhere the name appeared in stone, it was cut out.
  • Images destroyed or recarved. Statues were pulled down, and marble portraits were frequently reworked into a successor's likeness. Bronze went to the furnace.
  • Coinage withdrawn. Coins bearing the condemned face were called in and melted, though hoard evidence shows this was never comprehensive.
  • Records struck. The name removed from the consular lists, from acts, from the calendar.
  • The name itself forbidden. In severe cases, descendants and relatives were barred from using it, and the birthday was declared inauspicious.

Suetonius describes the aftermath of Domitian's assassination in 96 CE: senators arriving with ladders, tearing his portraits down on the spot and smashing them, and voting to erase his inscriptions everywhere. Pliny the Younger, writing as a contemporary who was there, describes the same scene with open pleasure — the axes going into the faces, the imperial features struck off.

A note on the term

Damnatio memoriae is not a phrase Romans used. It comes from early modern legal scholarship, and it imposes a tidy unified concept on what was in practice an ad hoc, negotiated and highly variable set of penalties. Some targets got everything. Some got a few statues pulled down. Some were condemned and then quietly rehabilitated later. The Latin sources talk about specific measures, not a doctrine.

Geta, and the most thorough attempt

Caracalla's erasure of his brother is the case where the machinery ran at full power, and it is well documented precisely because it failed so visibly.

On the Arch of the Argentarii in Rome — a small monument put up by the money-changers' guild — the family is shown sacrificing. Geta's figure has been cut out of the relief, leaving an obvious rectangular gap in the composition with the surviving figures gesturing toward nothing. The dedicatory inscription has lines hacked away.

Papyri from Egypt show his name blotted out of official documents. Inscriptions across the empire have the gap. Ancient sources report that saying his name became dangerous, and Cassius Dio claims men were killed for possessing his image or mourning him.

And the result, eighteen centuries later, is that Geta is one of the better-known short-reigned emperors, chiefly because of the erasure. We can list where his name was removed, which monuments carry the gaps, and roughly how quickly the order propagated across the provinces. The deletion is a documented event with a paper trail.

The structural problem

The paradox is not subtle, and Roman elites cannot have missed it.

An erased inscription is not a blank inscription. It is an inscription with a conspicuous scar in it, cut into a legible sentence, in a public place, surrounded by intact text that explains the context. Anyone literate walking past a monument with a chiselled gap knows immediately that someone has been removed and can usually work out who from the remaining words.

Recarved statues are similar. Sculptors reworking a portrait into a successor's face left traces — proportions that no longer fit, hair recut over a different original, drill work at odd depths. Portrait specialists identify reworked heads routinely.

This is why the current scholarly consensus, argued most fully by Harriet Flower, has largely abandoned the idea that these sanctions were literal attempts at oblivion. The purpose was performance. The point of the empty space was that people should see it.

Read that way, the sanction becomes intelligible as politics. The Senate, or the successor, is making a public statement: this person's honours were illegitimate, this regime is repudiated, and the people who did the chiselling have chosen a side. A dishonoured absence is a stronger political message than a name nobody notices. The gap is the announcement.

The Egyptian versions, which are not the same

Egypt did this earlier and on a larger scale, though the motives look different.

Hatshepsut, who ruled as pharaoh in the fifteenth century BCE, had her images defaced and her name removed from monuments and king lists. The obvious reading — a resentful successor lashing out — runs into a problem: the erasures appear to have begun roughly two decades after her death, under Thutmose III, and they were selective rather than total. Immediate personal spite does not fit a twenty-year delay. Current interpretations lean toward dynastic housekeeping: securing an unambiguous male succession by removing an anomaly from the official line, without particular animus.

Akhenaten received harsher treatment. After the collapse of his religious revolution, his city was abandoned, his monuments dismantled, and his name omitted from later king lists, which skip from his predecessor to Horemheb as though the intervening decades did not occur. His temple blocks were reused as rubble fill inside later structures — which is why thousands of them survive in excellent condition, protected from weather for three thousand years by the very people trying to eliminate them.

Egyptologists recovered the Amarna period substantially from that fill. The erasure preserved the evidence.

The uncomfortable part

All of this concerns the failures, because failures are what leave traces. Which raises a question with no available answer.

We know about Geta because the erasure was clumsy, hurried, and applied across an empire too large to police. We know about Akhenaten because his stones were reused rather than ground down. Every case discussed here is a case where the procedure did not work.

There is no way to identify the cases where it did. A successful erasure produces no gap to notice, no reworked portrait to detect, no anomaly in the record — because a genuinely complete removal would also remove the evidence that anything had been removed.

What we can say is that the ancient world had both the administrative capacity and the political will to attempt this, repeatedly, over millennia. Augustus burned two thousand books of unauthorised prophecy in a single operation and we know of it only from a passing line in Suetonius.

The blank spaces we can see are the ones where somebody was in a hurry.
