In the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore, a bronze right hand sits raised in blessing, the first two fingers extended, the rest curled. It could almost be a Christian gesture, except for what's growing on it. A pine cone tops the thumb. A snake climbs the wrist. Somewhere among the fingers, if the piece is like most of its kind, there's a small lizard, or a toad, or a ram's head, or a tiny woman nursing a child. Nobody looking at it for the first time guesses what it was for.

It was made for the god Sabazios, and it is one of roughly eighty such hands that survive from the Roman world, all carrying some version of the same crowded, unexplained menagerie. Sabazios never became one of the big-name mystery cults the way Isis or Mithras did, but his worshippers left more physical evidence behind than most of the cults that did.

A gesture covered in animals

The hands are cast in the position Romans called the benedictio latina, the same two-fingered blessing that later Christian art borrowed for Christ and the saints. Some were mounted on poles for processions, others fixed to sceptres or set up as standalone votive offerings inside shrines. Maarten Vermaseren catalogued the surviving examples in 1983 for the first volume of the Corpus Cultus Iovis Sabazii, the reference project that still anchors nearly everything written on the cult, and the geographic spread is startling for a god almost nobody today has heard of: Italy, the Balkans, Roman Britain, and, as later finds pushed the map further, the Ukrainian steppe.

The symbols repeat with enough consistency to look deliberate and not enough to make a single confident reading. Pine cone, snake, lizard, frog, turtle, a pair of scales, a caduceus, sometimes a ram's head at the wrist. Underworld associations run through most of it, tying Sabazios to the same chthonic territory that Dionysos and, later, the Roman cult of Cybele occupied. But no ancient writer sat down and explained what the pine cone meant on this particular hand, for this particular god, and the absence leaves every modern account working from the object outward rather than from a text inward.

The expulsion of 139 BCE

The one moment Sabazios breaks into Roman political history is an edict most people have never heard of. In his collection of memorable deeds and sayings, the first-century writer Valerius Maximus records that the praetor Cornelius Hispalus, in 139 BCE, ordered the Jews out of Rome and back to their homes for trying to "infect Roman customs" with the worship of Jupiter Sabazius. The same passage, in the same breath, expels a group of astrologers he calls Chaldeans.

Put like that, it reads as a straightforward record of Jewish worshippers of a Phrygian god getting thrown out of the capital, one more entry in a long Roman habit of policing foreign cults that would later fall hardest on the Bacchanalia. Eugene Lane's 1979 re-examination in the Journal of Roman Studies made the case that it is nothing of the kind: the text as it survives has almost certainly folded together two separate expulsions, of astrologers and of Jews, and the Sabazius detail belongs to a confusion in the manuscript tradition rather than to anything the Jewish community of second-century BCE Rome was actually doing. There is no other evidence, from any period, of Jews worshipping Sabazios. What there is, later on, is a resemblance in sound: the Hebrew title Yahweh Sabaoth, "Lord of Hosts," sitting close enough to "Sabazios" that a Roman ear untrained in either language could plausibly mistake one for the other, and a few ancient and modern writers have taken that resemblance and built an identification out of it that most specialists in the region's religion now reject. Jewish communities are barely attested in the parts of Anatolia where Sabazios was native this early, which leaves the sound-alike theory short of the population it needs.

Phrygian, Thracian, or both

Even setting the Rome episode aside, Sabazios resists being pinned to one place. For decades the standard view made him purely Phrygian, a local Anatolian god absorbed into Greek religion as an eastern form of Zeus. Later scholarship, working from Thracian evidence, has argued the reverse or something in between: a mountain god shared by Phrygians and Thracians alike from a common ancestor culture, showing up with a chthonic character in Thrace and a skyward one once Phrygian worshippers reshaped him. Nobody has closed the argument, and the bronze hands don't help settle it. The same pine cone and the same snake turn up on hands found hundreds of miles apart, telling us the symbol set travelled a very long way. They don't tell us where it started.

What's left, in the end, is the object in the museum case: a hand raised as if mid-blessing, decorated with creatures whose exact meaning went out of use along with the god they served, expelled once by a Roman magistrate for reasons his own source can't fully explain, and still standing in bronze, fingers curled, two thousand years later.
