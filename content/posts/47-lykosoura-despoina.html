In the National Archaeological Museum in Athens, behind glass, is a strip of carved marble no wider than a scarf: a row of small figures with human bodies and the heads of animals, a bull, a horse, a ram, dancing in a line. It once hung as the veil on a cult statue six metres tall, inside a temple in the hills of southern Arcadia, at a sanctuary whose central goddess most Greeks were not permitted to name.

Pausanias, who saw that statue intact in the second century CE, tells us the goddess had a real name. He also tells us he isn't going to write it down.

A name too dangerous to say

Everyone who wrote about her called her Despoina, "the Mistress," a title rather than a name, the same word Greek used for the lady of a household. Pausanias records that the uninitiated were forbidden to hear her actual name, and elsewhere in his book he states plainly that he keeps this kind of secret on purpose, out of respect for the rites themselves. These small local mystery cults rarely explain themselves, and Despoina's is no exception. So a goddess whose myth made her the daughter of Demeter and Poseidon, born in one telling in the shape of a horse, survives in our sources only as the word Greeks used instead of the thing they were not supposed to say.

Six metres of stone in the wrong material

What remains of the cult image is the work of Damophon of Messene, a sculptor active in the middle of the second century BCE, and it was enormous: Despoina and Demeter enthroned side by side, Artemis and the Titan Anytos standing at their flanks, the whole group filling the back wall of the temple. Pausanias says it was carved from a single block of marble. It wasn't. What survives, heads, hands and drapery fragments, now in Athens, shows an acrolithic technique: marble for the visible skin, a wooden core underneath, painted and robed so that from the front it read as solid stone. Even a careful eyewitness standing in the room can get the engineering of a statue wrong while getting everything else about the cult right.

The Greek Archaeological Society, under Panagiotis Kavvadias, began digging at the site in 1889 and recovered the group that same summer, not intact but in pieces: the heads of Demeter, Artemis and Anytos, and a fragment of Despoina's mantle, with no complete body surviving for any of the four figures. What is on display in Athens today is assembled from those fragments.

The veil is the part that keeps people arguing. Carved along it, in bands, are eagles and thunderbolts, a marine procession, and then the animal-headed dancers, bull, horse, ram and others too worn to name, human bodies topped with the wrong heads, moving together in what looks like a ritual line. Nobody has produced a reading of the scene that other scholars accept without reservation. It may show a specific rite. It may show mythic beings rather than human initiates. The stone does not caption itself.

How old is a sanctuary allowed to be

Pausanias calls Lykosoura the oldest city the sun ever looked down on, older even than Egyptian Thebes. Modern excavation has found nothing at the site earlier than the fourth century BCE, right around the date the region's communities were merged by force into the new city of Megalopolis. No inscription and no earlier author mentions Despoina, or her mysteries, before the second century BCE. That gap, between Pausanias's claim of primordial antiquity and the silence of every source before him, is the real argument.

Alaya Palamidis has made the sharpest case that the silence means roughly what it looks like: that Despoina's cult, and the pan-Arcadian identity it was built to express, was assembled by Megalopolis out of older regional material after the synoecism of 371–370 BCE, then given a founding myth old enough to outdate everyone else's. Madeleine Jost, whose 1985 survey of Arcadian religion remains the standard reference for the whole region, answers that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence: large parts of the site have never been excavated, and comparable sanctuaries at Tegea and on Mount Lykaion turned out to be centuries older than expected once someone finally dug there. In a piece the two scholars published together in 2020, Jost's conclusion was to suspend judgement rather than settle it. That is where the question still sits.

The one thing that survived intact

A sacred law found at the sanctuary regulates the festival in the same flat, procedural style as the one governing the mysteries at Andania: dress, conduct, penalties. What it does not do, any more than the Andania text does, is explain the rite. Between a statue Pausanias got partly wrong, a name nobody would write down, and a founding date two respected specialists still cannot settle, Despoina remains exactly what her title promised: present in the record mainly as the shape of what wasn't said about her, in much the same way the gods of Samothrace resist being named at all.
