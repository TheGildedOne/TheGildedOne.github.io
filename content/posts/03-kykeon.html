In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, the grieving goddess arrives at Eleusis in disguise, sits in silence, and is eventually offered wine. She refuses it. She asks instead for barley meal and water, flavoured with pennyroyal — a mint. The mixture is prepared, she drinks it, and the cult is founded.

That drink is the kykeon, and it is one of the very few concrete physical details we possess about the Eleusinian rite. The initiates fasted, walked fourteen miles, arrived at night, and broke their fast with it before entering the hall.

The obvious question has been asked at least since the eighteenth century. It became a serious research programme in 1978, and it has been unresolved ever since.

The Road to Eleusis

The 1978 book was the work of three people with unusually well-matched expertise, which is part of why it landed as hard as it did.

R. Gordon Wasson was a banker turned amateur mycologist who had spent decades documenting ritual mushroom use in Mexico and had become the most prominent figure in the study of psychoactive plants in religion. Albert Hofmann was the Swiss chemist who first synthesised LSD, working from ergot alkaloids — there was no living person who understood ergot chemistry better. Carl Ruck was, and remained, a professor of classics at Boston University.

Their argument runs in four steps:

  1. The kykeon was made from barley grown on the Rarian plain at Eleusis — a field with specific mythological significance to the cult.
  2. Barley in a Mediterranean climate is routinely infected by Claviceps purpurea, the ergot fungus, which produces visible dark bodies in place of grains.
  3. Ergot contains a mixture of alkaloids. Some are severely toxic vasoconstrictors. Others — notably ergonovine and ergine — are psychoactive, and are related to the compound Hofmann had worked from.
  4. The toxic alkaloids are poorly soluble in water; some psychoactive ones are more soluble. A simple water extraction, they proposed, could therefore yield a visionary preparation while leaving the poison behind.

The conclusion is dramatic and, in its way, elegant: the priesthood at Eleusis had discovered and preserved a reliable pharmacological technique, and guarded it as the central secret of the cult for two thousand years.

What genuinely supports it

Several things do, and it is worth being fair to them before turning to the objections.

The uniformity of the experience. Whatever happened in the Telesterion happened to thousands of people at once, produced comparable effects across widely different individuals, and did so reliably enough that the cult's reputation survived for two millennia. Purely psychological staging is capable of a great deal, but reliable mass effect on that scale is a demanding requirement.

The refusal of wine. The founding myth goes out of its way to have Demeter reject the standard Greek ritual drink in favour of this specific alternative. That is an odd detail to invent unless the drink mattered.

The secrecy of the preparation. If the secret were purely a piece of theatre or a doctrine, it is hard to see why the recipe would need protecting. If it were a technique, protection makes obvious sense — and would explain why the knowledge died completely rather than leaking gradually.

The Iberian residues. This is the strongest new evidence, and it arrived long after the original book. At Mas Castellar de Pontós in Catalonia — a settlement with a Greek-influenced ritual complex — analysis identified ergot alkaloids in residue from a ritual drinking vessel and in human dental calculus. That establishes something the 1978 authors could not: that ergot was, in fact, being consumed in a ritual context in the ancient western Mediterranean.

What genuinely undermines it

The chemistry is contested, and it is the load-bearing claim. The entire hypothesis rests on the separation step — that ordinary water extraction reliably delivers the psychoactive alkaloids and excludes the toxic ones. Pharmacologists have disputed both the selectivity and the dose consistency. Ergot content varies enormously between infected heads, between fields and between seasons. A preparation that depends on the ratio of two alkaloid families in wild fungal infection is not obviously something you can standardise for several thousand people a night without producing casualties.

The casualties are missing. Ergotism was a well-known and horrifying condition in medieval Europe — gangrene, convulsions, limbs lost. If Eleusis was administering ergot preparations annually at scale for centuries, some proportion of failures ought to have surfaced somewhere in a very gossipy documentary record. Nothing does.

There is no ancient description of any technique. The hypothesis requires a sophisticated, deliberately maintained preparation method. No ancient source describes one, hints at one, or refers to the priesthood possessing specialist knowledge of that kind.

And no residue evidence from Eleusis exists. This is the plain state of things. The Iberian finds show possibility, not practice at this site.

The methodological problem underneath all this

Notice how the hypothesis is structured. The secret was perfectly kept, therefore no evidence of it survives, therefore the absence of evidence is exactly what the theory predicts. That is a genuinely awkward position — a claim insulated from disconfirmation by the very thing it is trying to explain. It does not make the hypothesis wrong. It does mean it can never be closed by argument alone, only by physical evidence from the site.

The possibility both sides tend to skip

There is a third answer that neither camp finds satisfying, and it deserves more weight than it usually gets: the staging may simply have been sufficient.

Reconstruct the nine days as a piece of engineering rather than as religion. The candidate has been fasting. They have walked fourteen miles in a crowd of thousands, chanting, since morning. They have been ritually insulted by masked strangers at a bridge. They arrive after dark. They have been anticipating this for at least six months, since the Lesser Mysteries, and in many cases for years. Everyone they know who has been through it has refused to tell them what happens.

Then they are taken into an enclosed hall, packed in the dark with thousands of others, subjected to sound and torchlight in an architecture built for exactly this — and at the climax, a great light in the blackness, and a figure appearing.

Modern research on fasting, sleep deprivation, sensory manipulation, crowd effects and expectancy is fairly clear that this combination produces powerful and reasonably consistent altered states without any pharmacology at all. Every element of the sequence is attested. None of it requires a drug.

The ergot hypothesis, in other words, may be solving a problem that does not need solving.

Where this actually stands

Honest summary, without a thumb on the scale:

  • The kykeon existed, and initiates drank it. Certain.
  • Its basic composition was barley, water and pennyroyal. Well attested, from the Hymn.
  • Ergot infects Mediterranean barley and was consumed ritually somewhere in the ancient western Mediterranean. Established.
  • Ergot was deliberately used at Eleusis. Possible. Not demonstrated.
  • A reliable water-based separation of psychoactive from toxic alkaloids was known and practised. Disputed on chemical grounds, and unattested.
  • The experience required a drug at all. Not established, and arguably unnecessary.

What would settle it is unglamorous: residue analysis on vessels securely excavated from the sanctuary, published with proper controls. Nothing else will. Until then this is a hypothesis with real supporting circumstantial evidence and a real unresolved objection at its core, which is a considerably more interesting thing than either its advocates or its debunkers usually allow.

Meanwhile the underlying fact holds. The rite was kept secret so thoroughly that we are reduced to arguing about the chemistry of a drink mentioned in a hymn, because it is the only physical evidence anybody left us.
