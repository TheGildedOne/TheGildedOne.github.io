On the inside of the Arch of Titus in Rome, a line of men in laurel wreaths carry the seven-branched lampstand of the Temple in Jerusalem through the streets in triumph. The city fell in 70 CE. The arch went up after Titus died in 81.

Somewhere in those same years, a Jewish poet answered it in Greek verse. A leader from Rome, he wrote, would burn the Temple. And then, when fire burst from a cleft in the earth in Italy and burned many cities, the world should recognise the anger of God, because they had destroyed the blameless people.

Vesuvius erupted in 79. The poet didn't sign his name. He wrote as the Sibyl.

Twelve books in Homer's metre

That passage is in Book 4 of the Sibylline Oracles, a collection of Greek hexameter prophecies, written in the metre of Homer and spoken throughout by the Sibyl, the frenzied prophetess of pagan tradition whom Virgil placed in a cave at Cumae. They foretell the rise and fall of empires, earthquakes, plagues and the end of the world.

There are twelve books, numbered 1 to 8 and 11 to 14. There is no 9 or 10: when the later books turned up in manuscripts in the nineteenth century, the material that would have filled those numbers just repeated books already printed. A Byzantine editor, probably in the sixth century, put the collection together and wrote a prose preface. The poems themselves are much older, and they aren't one work. They're a pile of separate texts by separate authors, Jewish and then Christian, spanning several centuries.

And they are not the Sibylline Books of Rome. Those were the state's own oracles, kept on the Capitol and read only by a board of priests, burned in 83 BCE, recollected, and finally destroyed. Nobody outside that board ever read them. That is the point. The Sibyl's name carried enormous authority and the real books were locked away, so anyone could borrow her voice. Plenty did. In 12 BCE Augustus burned two thousand prophetic books circulating in Rome.

The Sibyl as Noah's daughter-in-law

The oldest layer is Book 3. It retells the Tower of Babel and the flood, warns the nations to give up idols, and promises a golden age when a king sent from the sun brings peace. At the end the Sibyl explains herself. The Greeks call her mad, she says, and say she comes from Erythrae. In fact she was on the Ark. She is Noah's daughter-in-law.

It's a clever move. The Sibyl is the oldest prophet the pagans have, and here she turns out to be part of the family of Israel, delivering the God of Israel's message to the Greeks in their own sacred metre. It worked well enough that Josephus, in the late first century, cites her on the Tower of Babel as a respectable outside witness.

When it was written, and where, is the longest-running argument in the field. Book 3 refers three times to a "seventh king" of Egypt of Greek descent, in whose reign good things will come. John Collins took that as a real Ptolemy, probably Ptolemy VI Philometor, and placed the core of the book among Jews in Egypt in the mid-second century BCE. Rieuwerd Buitenwerf argued in 2003 that nobody in antiquity numbered the Ptolemies, that "seventh" is symbolic, and that the book was written in the Roman province of Asia between about 80 and 40 BCE. The same verses support both.

Nero comes back

Books 4 and 5 are also Jewish, and angrier. They were written after the Temple fell. Book 5 is obsessed with Nero, the matricide who fled beyond the Euphrates and will return to wreck the world. After Nero died in 68 there really were rumours he'd survived, and at least one impostor drew followers in the east.

Book 5 also flatters a later emperor in code: a silver-haired man whose name is a sea, which means Hadrian, after the Adriatic. The author apparently wrote before Hadrian crushed the Bar Kokhba revolt, or he'd hardly have called him excellent.

The acrostic

Then the Christians arrived. Books 6 and 7 are Christian, Book 8 is partly so, and Books 1 and 2 are disputed. Collins saw a Jewish original under Christian additions. Jane Lightfoot, in her 2007 commentary, reads them as the work of a second-century Christian from start to finish.

Book 8 holds the famous piece: thirty-four lines on the Last Judgement whose first letters spell, in Greek, "Jesus Christ, Son of God, Saviour, Cross". Augustine says a former proconsul named Flaccianus showed him the Greek in a manuscript, and he quotes a Latin version in the City of God. Lactantius drew on the Sibyl constantly. The speech attributed to Constantine, addressed to the Christian faithful, quotes the acrostic in full.

That speech also admits the problem. Many people, it says, suspect a Christian with a gift for verse wrote the lines and they're a forgery. Its answer is that Cicero had read the poem and translated it into Latin. Cicero does say that Sibylline verses were written as acrostics, which to him proved they were composed with care, not in a frenzy. He never mentions this one. The pagan critic Celsus had already mocked Christians as "Sibyllists" and accused them of slipping blasphemies into her verses. On this point he was right.

Nobody in the Middle Ages much minded. The Latin hymn of judgement, the Dies Irae, names the Sibyl alongside King David as a witness to the end of the world. On the Sistine Chapel ceiling, Michelangelo painted five Sibyls among the prophets of Israel.
