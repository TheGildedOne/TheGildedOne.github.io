The maid's name was Abra, and she was supposed to be watching the door.

It was a night in early December, 62 BCE. Julius Caesar's house was full of women, and Caesar wasn't in it. He couldn't be. Every year the wives of Rome's senior magistrates held a rite for the Bona Dea, the Good Goddess, on behalf of the whole Roman people, and every male had to leave. Juvenal claims even the paintings of men on the walls had to be veiled.

This year it was at Caesar's, because he was praetor and chief priest of Rome. His wife Pompeia was the hostess. His mother Aurelia was there too. So were the Vestal Virgins.

So was Publius Clodius Pulcher, about thirty years old, beardless, and dressed as a lute-girl.

Caught by his voice

Plutarch tells the story in his Life of Caesar. Abra was in on it and let him in, then went off to find Pompeia. She took too long. Clodius got bored of waiting where she'd left him and wandered into the house.

One of Aurelia's attendants spotted the shy musician in the corridor and asked her to come and play. Clodius refused. She dragged him forward and asked his name, and he answered. The voice did it. She ran back screaming that there was a man in the house.

Aurelia stopped the rite at once and had the sacred objects covered. She locked the doors and searched the house by torchlight. They found him hiding in Abra's room, and threw him out. The women went home that night and told their husbands.

That's Plutarch, writing a century and a half later. The nearest thing we have to a live report is Cicero, who wrote to his friend Atticus on the first of January. His version is shorter and drier: Clodius had been caught in women's clothes at Caesar's house while the sacrifice was being made for the people. The Vestals repeated the rite. Caesar had divorced his wife.

Why Clodius went in, nobody knew then, and we don't now. The gossip said he was having an affair with Pompeia. That's all it ever was.

The trial

In a republic that ran on the gods' goodwill, wrecking a rite performed for the state wasn't a prank. It was sacrilege. The Senate pushed for a trial, and it came in 61 BCE.

Clodius said he'd been out of town at Interamna that night. Cicero ruined the alibi by testifying that Clodius had called on him in Rome the same day. Plutarch says Cicero's wife Terentia pushed him into it. Clodius never forgave him.

Caesar, the injured husband, was called and said he knew nothing against the man. So why, he was asked, had he divorced Pompeia? Because, he said, his wife ought not even to be under suspicion. It's the origin of "Caesar's wife must be above suspicion", and it's a beautifully slippery answer. Plutarch says some thought he meant it, and others that he was keeping Clodius's supporters sweet.

The jury acquitted, 31 to 25. Cicero told Atticus exactly what he thought had happened. The jurors had asked for a bodyguard during the trial, and Cicero quotes the senator Catulus asking one of them afterwards whether they'd wanted it to protect their money. Cicero blamed a bald man for putting up the cash, which is usually taken to mean Crassus.

Clodius got his revenge. As tribune in 58 BCE he drove Cicero into exile.

What happened inside

Here's the strange thing. This is among the best-documented scandals of the Roman Republic, and we still barely know what the women were doing when they were interrupted.

The fragments come from Plutarch and from the late antique writer Macrobius. The house was decked with vine-leaves and plants, but myrtle was banned. A sow was sacrificed. There was music, which is why a lute-girl could get in. There was wine, but the women called it "milk", and the jar it came in a "honey-pot".

Macrobius explains the myrtle with a myth. Faunus wanted his daughter, the goddess, and got her drunk; when she still refused him, he beat her with myrtle switches. It reads like a story invented to explain a rule nobody understood any more.

And then there's Juvenal, who describes the rite as a drunken orgy, women howling and stripping, and asks which altar these days doesn't have its Clodius. It's satire. It's about as reliable as a tabloid, and it's been quoted as evidence for centuries.

Every one of these writers was a man. Not one could legally have seen the rite. Women who did see it left nothing. H. S. Versnel set it beside the Greek Thesmophoria, another festival for women only, and boiled the Bona Dea's oddities down to "wine, myrtle, serpents and female modesty blemished". That's about as close as the evidence lets anyone get.

The goddess outside the house

The secrecy was real, but it wasn't the whole cult. Bona Dea also had a temple on the Aventine, with healing herbs and tame snakes, and an ordinary following. When H. H. J. Brouwer gathered all her inscriptions in 1989, around a third of the dedications turned out to come from men, mostly freedmen and slaves. Some called themselves her ministers.

Ariadne Staples argued that the December rite was about categories as much as gods: an elite ceremony where the city's leading women acted for Rome, with the wine disguised so it belonged to the women and not to Bacchus. Rome had already shown what it thought of night rites it couldn't see, when it crushed the Bacchanalia in 186 BCE. This one survived because it was the state's own. Whoever commissioned the frescoes in the Villa of the Mysteries at Pompeii put a woman's initiation on the walls, and we can't read that either.

Clodius didn't last. He was killed in a brawl on the Appian Way in 52 BCE, near Bovillae.

There was a shrine of the Bona Dea right beside the spot.
