A ship is out at sea. On board are a handful of Pythagoreans and one man who has just proved something unbearable: that the diagonal of a square can't be measured against its side. No fraction will do it. The universe, which was supposed to be made of whole numbers, isn't. So his brothers throw him over the side.

That's the story as you'll hear it in a maths lesson, and the man is always Hippasus of Metapontum. It's a wonderful story. No ancient writer tells it.

What the sources actually say

The main witness is Iamblichus, a Syrian philosopher writing around 300 CE, roughly eight hundred years after Hippasus lived. He tells the drowning more than once, and it doesn't come out the same way twice.

In one passage Hippasus is named. He was a Pythagorean, Iamblichus says, who published how to make a sphere out of twelve pentagons, the regular dodecahedron, and took the credit for it. He died at sea as an impious man. The discovery really belonged, the Pythagoreans said, to "that man", which was how they referred to Pythagoras without saying his name.

So the one source that names Hippasus says his secret was a shape, not a number.

Much later in the same book, Iamblichus comes back to the subject and gives three versions. The man who first revealed the theory of commensurable and incommensurable quantities to the unworthy was so hated that the Pythagoreans expelled him. Others say the divine power punished whoever published the dodecahedron, and he drowned. Others again say the drowning happened to the one who revealed the irrational. In none of these is anyone named.

Pappus of Alexandria, in a commentary on Euclid that survives only in Arabic, also says the Pythagorean who first divulged the irrational died by drowning. He doesn't name him either.

Nobody throws anyone overboard. The gods drown him, or the sea simply does. The modern story takes Hippasus' name from one passage, the irrational numbers from another and the murder from nowhere, and joins them up.

A tomb for a living man

The detail that tends to get left out is the most interesting one. In the version where the betrayer is expelled, the Pythagoreans also built a tomb for him, as though he'd died.

That wasn't special treatment. Earlier in the book Iamblichus describes what happened to candidates who were rejected after their years of probation: they got back double the property they'd brought, and the community raised a tomb to them as if they were dead. If members met them afterwards, they behaved as though meeting a stranger.

It's quite possible that the most famous murder in the history of mathematics began as a ritual of expulsion: a man declared dead by his former friends, with a monument to prove it, while he went on living down the road. We don't know. But it would explain how a story about a tomb turned into a story about a death.

The man himself

What else do we know about Hippasus? Very little, and none of it involves secrets.

The earliest mention is in Aristotle, who lists Hippasus of Metapontum alongside Heraclitus as thinkers who made fire the first principle of everything. Diogenes Laertius reports that he left no writings, and in another place that he wrote a book to slander Pythagoras. A note on Plato's Phaedo describes an experiment of his with four bronze discs.

Iamblichus also makes him the founder of one of the two Pythagorean factions, the "learners" who did mathematics and the "listeners" who kept the master's sayings. Except that in another work he makes him the founder of the other one. Leonid Zhmud doubts the two factions existed in Hippasus' day at all.

Even his city wavers. Aristotle says Metapontum, a Greek colony on the gulf of Taranto whose temple of Hera still has fifteen columns standing. Other sources say Croton, or Sybaris.

Was there a secret at all?

The incommensurable was real, and it was a Greek discovery. Plato describes Theodorus of Cyrene proving the irrationality of square roots up to seventeen, which implies someone had done the square root of two before him. That someone may well have been a Pythagorean.

In 1945 Kurt von Fritz proposed a neat way to make Hippasus the man. The faces of a dodecahedron are pentagons, and in a regular pentagon the diagonal and the side are incommensurable. Draw the pentagram, the Pythagoreans' own symbol, and the problem is sitting right there. So perhaps the two stories are really one.

It's clever, and it may be right, and it is a hypothesis built to reconcile sources that don't agree. Wilbur Knorr, the historian of Greek geometry, thought the ancient evidence linking Hippasus to the irrational was too thin to bear it. Walter Burkert showed how much of the late picture of Pythagoras, the sage with a hidden doctrine, was built centuries after his death by Platonists who wanted him to have been one of their own. Some Romans did the same thing their own way, deciding their king Numa had been his pupil, though Livy pointed out the dates made it impossible, so that when Numa's coffin was dug up in 181 BCE, the books inside were said to be Pythagorean philosophy.

Secrecy in southern Italy was real enough. It's the region that produced the gold tablets buried with initiates, telling the dead what to say in the underworld. But a cult that hid its passwords from the uninitiated is not the same as a school of geometers murdering a colleague over a proof.

The drowning stories come from those people. What survives from Hippasus' own time is shorter. If the note on Plato is right, he took four bronze discs of the same width, made them different thicknesses in exact ratios, and struck them, and they rang in harmony.
