Lead Ptolemais, whom Aias bore, the daughter of Horigenes, to me. That is the opening line on a strip of lead found in a jar in Egypt, and it names its target the way ancient magic almost always did: not by her father, but by her mother.

Inside the same jar sat a small clay woman. Kneeling, hands bound behind her back, naked, and pushed through with thirteen copper-alloy needles: one in the scalp, one in each eye, one in each ear, one in the mouth, one in the chest, one in each hand, one in each foot, one in the pudenda, one in the anus.

Whoever made her was not improvising.

A jar, a tablet, a woman in clay

The find is catalogued today as Louvre E 27145, published by Sophie Kambitsis in 1976 and dated on the evidence of the handwriting to the third or fourth century CE, reportedly from Antinoopolis in Roman Egypt. It is unbaked clay, which means it was never meant to survive being handled, only to sit sealed in its jar doing its work in the dark.

The tablet names the man behind it as Sarapammon, son of a woman called Area. It names the target as Ptolemais, daughter of Horigenes and Aias. Naming a target by their mother rather than their father was standard practice in this kind of magic, inherited from older Egyptian ritual habits: a mother is certain in a way a father sometimes is not, and a spell that misidentifies its target is a spell wasted.

The needle placements are not random either. They match, point by point, a recipe that survives independently of this object, in a different find entirely, from a different magician's toolkit.

Kambitsis worked out the date from the handwriting on the tablet rather than from anything in the jar itself, which had lost whatever context its findspot might once have given it by the time it reached the museum. That gap between object and archaeology is common in this material: a find is often bought or handed in decades after being dug up, and the magic gets studied on its own terms because the dig report never existed or never survived.

A cookbook, and a customer

The Great Magical Papyrus of Paris, now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, contains a long Greek text known from its own heading as the Wondrous Spell for Binding a Lover, running to well over a hundred lines. It belongs to the same family of working manuals as the rest of that document: instructions, not narrative, written for a professional to follow on commission rather than for a single occasion.

The instructions call for a clay or wax figure of the bound woman, made kneeling with her arms twisted behind her, and thirteen bronze needles driven into named body parts in a fixed order, each one accompanied by a spoken formula invoking underworld powers by name.

The Louvre doll follows this almost to the letter. It is one of at least six surviving objects made to the same formula from Roman Egypt, which means Sarapammon, whoever he actually was, did not invent his own curse. He, or the magician he paid, ordered a known product and personalised it with two names.

What the tablet says to do to her

The wording is not restrained. Do not let her eat, drink, find rest, or sleep, it says, until she comes to Sarapammon. Drag her by the hair, by the guts, until she stops scorning him and gives herself to him alone.

Christopher Faraone's study of Greek love magic draws a sharp line through this material by gender. Spells commissioned by men, aimed at women who have not agreed to anything, tend toward exactly this register: torment until compliance, framed as passion. Spells associated with women run the other way, toward binding and calming an unfaithful or violent husband rather than inflaming him. The Louvre doll sits squarely in the first category. Nothing in it asks Ptolemais to feel anything resembling love. It asks her to be broken until she stops resisting.

Faraone's wider count of surviving erotic binding spells whose commissioner's gender can be identified finds the same pattern repeated across the corpus, not just in this one object. A man ordering the destruction of a woman's peace of mind until she gives in is the default shape of the genre, not an outlier the Louvre doll happens to represent.

What we cannot know about Ptolemais

Here the evidence runs out, and it is worth being honest about where. We do not know whether Ptolemais knew a curse existed with her name on it. We do not know whether she and Sarapammon had any prior relationship at all, or whether he had simply seen her and wanted her. We do not know whether the magician who made this object had a shelf of pre-formed dolls waiting for a name to be scratched onto the tablet, the way a modern print shop keeps templates on file.

What repeats across six separate finds is the formula, not the feeling. That does not make the violence in the words less real as an artefact of what Sarapammon wanted to believe he was capable of. It does mean we should not read this single object as a uniquely monstrous act. It was, by the evidence of its own repetition, a service someone in Roman Egypt could simply buy.

Still sealed, in a museum case

The doll and its tablet are displayed together now, in the Louvre's Egyptian collection, the jar that held them both catalogued alongside. Whatever Sarapammon paid for it, and whatever Ptolemais never knew about it, the two objects have not been separated since the day they went into the ground together.
