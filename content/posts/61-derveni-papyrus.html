On 15 January 1962, road builders working on the highway from Thessaloniki to Kavala cut into a cemetery at the pass of Derveni.

The archaeologists who came out found a cluster of graves, some of them rich. On the slabs covering one of them, Tomb A, lay the debris of the funeral pyre: ash, bits of bone, charred grave goods. And among it, some blackened lumps that didn't look like wood.

They were a book. A papyrus roll had gone onto the fire with the dead man. The flames burned away the lower half and turned the top to carbon, and carbon, unlike papyrus, survives in damp Macedonian soil. About two hundred and sixty fragments were picked apart and fitted together into twenty-six columns of text, all missing their bottoms.

The full official edition came out in 2006. It took forty-four years. In between, a provisional text appeared anonymously in a German journal in 1982, and scholars argued from that and from scraps published here and there. Nobody was willing to wait.

A commentary on Orpheus

The roll was copied around 340–320 BCE, which makes it the oldest surviving book in Europe. UNESCO put it on its Memory of the World register in 2015. The text is older than the copy, probably from the end of the fifth century BCE.

The first columns are about ritual. They mention the magi, offerings to the Erinyes, cakes and birds, and the souls of the dead, and they quote Heraclitus on the size of the sun. Then the author turns to his main business: a poem by Orpheus about how the gods came to be, which he quotes a line or two at a time and explains.

His method is allegory. Orpheus, he insists, speaks in riddles. When the poem talks about gods and their violent family history, it really means the physical world: elements separating, things coming together. Zeus, for instance, turns out to be air, and Mind. The commentator is a theologian reading a sacred poem through the physics of his day, a bit like someone explaining Genesis with a textbook open beside it.

The poem is the same family of Orphic writing that put instructions for the dead on the gold tablets buried in southern Italian graves. Its story is rather stranger.

What Zeus swallowed

In the Derveni theogony, power passes from Night to Ouranos to Kronos to Zeus. At the key moment Zeus swallows something, and everything that exists ends up inside him. He then brings the world out again, made new.

The trouble is the word for what he swallows: aidoion. As a noun it means genitals. As an adjective it means "revered".

Read it the first way, and Zeus swallows the phallus of Ouranos, cut off by Kronos a generation earlier. That's Betegh's reading, and it gives Zeus the generative power of creation in the most literal possible form. Read it the second way, as Martin West and Luc Brisson did, and he swallows "the revered one", the first-born god Protogonos, which is what happens in later Orphic poems. Brisson asked the obvious question of the phallus reading: why would Zeus wait a whole generation to swallow it? Vojtěch Hladký, in 2024, had Kronos do the swallowing instead. His reviewer, Mirjam Kotwick, wasn't convinced.

So the most famous line in Europe's oldest book is still a matter of grammar. Which is fitting, for an author who thought every word of Orpheus was a puzzle.

Who wrote it

The author never gives his name, and he has collected a lot of candidates.

Walter Burkert suggested Stesimbrotus of Thasos, a fifth-century writer on Homer. Charles Kahn proposed Euthyphro, the self-important seer Plato puts up against Socrates. Andrei Lebedev argues for the sophist Prodicus. And Richard Janko, the boldest of all, named Diagoras of Melos, the man Athens condemned around 415 BCE for mocking the Mysteries at Eleusis, and put a price on his head.

None of these has carried the field. Gábor Betegh, who rejects Janko's Diagoras in an appendix to his book, thinks the author was most likely a religious professional with Orphic loyalties.

There's a passage that cuts both ways. In column XX the author turns on people who go to a paid practitioner of rites to be initiated. They hand over the fee in advance, he says, and come away without understanding what they've seen, and without even the hope of understanding it. He sounds like someone who thought he could do the job properly himself. Or like someone who thought the whole business was a racket. Scholars have read it both ways.

Why put it on the fire?

We don't know who was buried in Tomb A. A man of some wealth, in Macedonia, in the time of Philip II, and that's about all.

We don't know why his book went on his pyre, either. Janko points out the plain possibility that it was waste paper used to get the fire lit. He also points out what's wrong with that: it went on as a complete roll, not torn up, which looks more like a precious possession burned with its owner. Glenn Most connected the text's cosmology with hopes for what came after death, which would make it a strange and fitting thing to burn. A book about the gods for a man who may have been an initiate, or kindling. The evidence allows either.

The roll is in the Archaeological Museum of Thessaloniki now, in pieces under glass. The tops of the columns, the only parts that survive, are the parts the fire reached first.
