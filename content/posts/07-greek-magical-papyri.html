Sometime in the 1820s, near Thebes in Upper Egypt, a large collection of papyrus rolls and codices came out of the ground. They passed through a dealer in Alexandria and into museum collections in Leiden, London, Paris and Berlin, where they sat for decades attracting very little attention.

What they contain is unlike anything else from the ancient world. Not a hostile outsider's description of magic, not a philosopher's theory about it, not a law banning it — the working documents themselves. Recipes. Procedures. Ingredient lists. Instructions written by practitioners for practitioners.

They're the largest body of ancient ritual instruction we have, and the reason is almost accidental: papyrus survives in the Egyptian climate and essentially nowhere else in the Mediterranean.

What the recipes actually cover

The range is broader and a lot more mundane than the reputation suggests.

  • Divination. The biggest category. Lamp divination, where a practitioner — often using a boy as a medium — stares into a flame in a dark room and reports what appears. Bowl divination with oil on water. Dream-sending, to get answers while asleep.
  • Protection. Amulets against fever, scorpions, headache, difficult childbirth, and hostile magic worked by other people.
  • Binding. Spells to silence an opponent in court, to fix a rival's tongue, to make someone sleepless until they come to you. This shades straight into the curse tablets found buried across the Roman world.
  • Household nuisances. Catching a thief. Winning at dice. Making a business prosper. Stopping a nosebleed.
  • Ascent. A handful of extraordinary texts for leaving the body and meeting a god directly.

The tone is technical throughout. These aren't devotional writings. They read like a trade manual: do this, then this, and if it doesn't work, here's the alternative procedure.

How a spell is built

Four components, remarkably consistent across hundreds of texts.

Materials, specified precisely. A lamp of a particular kind, not painted red. Ink made with myrrh. A strip of clean linen, a lead sheet, a piece of unfired pot. Sometimes something belonging to the target — hair, thread from clothing.

Purity requirements. Avoid certain foods. Stay away from other people for a set number of days. Work at a specific hour, often when a particular star rises.

The formula, which is where it gets strange. Alongside recognisable divine names — Greek, Egyptian, and a striking amount of Jewish material, with Iao, Sabaoth and Adonai everywhere — the texts are dense with voces magicae: long strings of syllables in no language at all. Some are palindromes. They're written to be said exactly as given.

The compulsion. This surprises modern readers most. The practitioner doesn't beg. They command, and they back the command with threats — claiming knowledge of the god's true name, or warning of cosmic consequences if refused. The relationship is leverage, not devotion.

The syncretism has been read as ignorant jumble: a single spell might invoke a Greek god, an Egyptian one, the Jewish divine name and a run of nonsense syllables in three lines. It's better read as professional pragmatism. The practitioner isn't picking a religion. They're assembling everything with a reputation for working, from every tradition available in Roman Egypt at once.

The ritual for meeting a god

Buried in the great Paris codex is a text unlike its neighbours, known since 1903 as the Mithras Liturgy.

It's a first-person set of instructions for an ascent. The practitioner prepares, breathes in a prescribed pattern, and then experiences a sequence: the disc of the sun opening, doors flying apart, a wind rising, a passage through the elements, and finally an immense figure appearing. There are things to say at each stage — and, critically, things to say when challenged by the beings met on the way, so you can identify yourself and keep going.

Two things follow, and both matter.

That structure — a journey, guardians, a challenge, a memorised correct answer — is precisely the structure of the Orphic gold tablets buried with the dead, centuries earlier and a thousand miles west. Here it's applied to someone still alive.

And the Mithraic attribution is genuinely contested. The text names Mithras but doesn't match anything known from Mithraic archaeology, and specialists have argued the name may be borrowed prestige. It might be the closest thing we have to a mystery-cult liturgy. It might be an Egyptian ritualist invoking a fashionable foreign god.

The genuinely unpleasant part

It would be dishonest to present this as harmless antiquarian curiosity, because some of it isn't.

A substantial group are coercive attraction spells, and the language is brutal. They ask gods to deprive a named woman of sleep, food and rest until she comes to the operator. They specify her mother's name for identification. These aren't requests for affection. They're requests for the destruction of someone's ability to refuse.

And they weren't theoretical. A clay figurine now in the Louvre, pierced with thirteen bronze needles and buried in a jar with a lead tablet carrying exactly such a text, matches a procedure in the papyri closely enough to show people carried out the whole thing as written.

Why we have these and nothing else

There's a puzzle here. The mystery cults kept their secrets for two thousand years. These practitioners wrote everything down.

The difference is function, not discipline. A mystery initiation happened to you once, in a hall, in a crowd, and its power depended on being undergone. A magical operation had to be executed correctly, in order, with the right words, possibly years after it was learned. Procedures need documentation. Experiences don't.

There's also the question of what happened to such books elsewhere. Owning them was dangerous — magic was prosecutable under Roman law, Apuleius stood trial for exactly that, and later authorities burned magical texts as policy. Egypt gave us a corpus not because Egyptians were more magical than anyone else, but because sand preserves papyrus and these particular rolls were buried before anyone could burn them.

So the honest description isn't "the magical literature of the ancient world." It's the fraction that ended up somewhere dry.
