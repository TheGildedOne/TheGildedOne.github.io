Sometime in the 1820s, in or near Thebes in Upper Egypt, a substantial collection of papyrus rolls and codices came out of the ground. They passed through the hands of a diplomat and dealer in Alexandria named Jean d'Anastasy, and from there into museum collections in Leiden, London, Paris and Berlin, where they sat for decades attracting relatively little attention.

What they contain is unlike anything else that survives from the ancient world. Not a description of magic by a hostile outsider, not a philosopher's theory about it, and not a law banning it — but the working documents themselves. Recipes. Procedures. Ingredient lists. Instructions written by practitioners for practitioners, with the wear and correction marks of things actually used.

They are the single largest body of ancient ritual instruction we possess, and the reason they exist is almost accidental: papyrus survives in the Egyptian climate, and nowhere else in the Mediterranean does.

What the recipes actually cover

The range is wider and considerably more mundane than the reputation suggests. A rough taxonomy:

  • Divination. The largest category by volume. Lamp divination, in which a practitioner — often using a young boy as a medium — stares into a flame in a darkened room and reports what appears. Bowl divination, using oil on water. Dream-sending, to obtain answers while asleep.
  • Protection. Amulets against fever, scorpions, headache, difficult childbirth, and hostile magic worked by others.
  • Binding and compulsion. Spells to silence an opponent in court, to make someone sleepless until they come to you, to fix a rival's tongue. This category shades directly into the curse tablets found buried across the Roman world.
  • Practical nuisances. Spells to catch a thief, to win at dice, to make a business prosper, to stop a nosebleed.
  • Ascent. A small number of extraordinary texts describing rituals for leaving the body and encountering a god directly.

The tone throughout is technical. These are not devotional writings. They read like a craftsman's manual: do this, then this, and if the operation fails, here is the alternative procedure.

The machinery of a spell

A typical operation has four components, and the consistency across hundreds of texts is one of the corpus's most striking features.

Materials. Specified with real precision. A lamp of a particular kind, not painted red. Ink compounded from myrrh. A strip of clean linen, or a lead sheet, or a piece of an unfired pot. Sometimes an object connected to the target — hair, thread from clothing.

Purity requirements. Abstain from certain foods. Keep apart from other people for a set number of days. Perform at a specific hour, often at the rising of a particular star.

The formula. This is where the papyri become genuinely strange. Alongside recognisable divine names — Greek, Egyptian, and a striking quantity of Jewish material, with Iao, Sabaoth and Adonai appearing constantly — the texts are dense with voces magicae: long strings of syllables belonging to no language. Some are palindromes, constructed to read the same in both directions. They are written to be pronounced exactly as given.

The compulsion. Most surprising to modern readers is the tone taken toward the divine. The practitioner does not usually beg. They command, and back the command with threats — asserting knowledge of the god's true name, or warning of cosmic consequences if the operation is refused. The relationship is one of leverage, not devotion.

On the syncretism

A single spell might invoke a Greek god, an Egyptian one, the Jewish divine name, and a string of untranslatable syllables, all in three lines. This has been read as ignorance or superstitious jumble. It is better read as a working professional's approach to efficacy: the practitioner is not choosing a religion. They are assembling everything with a reputation for functioning, from every tradition available in Roman Egypt at once.

The ritual for meeting a god

Buried in the longest of the papyri — the great Paris codex — is a text quite unlike its neighbours. It has been known since Albrecht Dieterich's edition of 1903 as the Mithras Liturgy, and it is one of the most remarkable documents to survive from antiquity.

It is a first-person set of instructions for an ascent. The practitioner prepares, breathes in a prescribed pattern, and then experiences a sequence: the disc of the sun opening, doors flying apart, a wind rising, a passage through the elements, and finally the appearance of an immense figure. There are things to say at each stage — and, critically, things to say when challenged by the beings encountered on the way, so the operator can identify themselves and continue.

Two observations follow, and both matter.

First, that structure — a journey, guardians, challenges, and a memorised correct answer — is exactly the structure of the Orphic gold tablets buried with the dead, several centuries earlier and a thousand miles away. Here it is applied to a living person.

Second, the Mithraic attribution is genuinely contested. The text names Mithras, but it does not match what is known from Mithraic archaeology, and specialists have argued at length that the name may be borrowed prestige rather than evidence of the cult itself. It may be the closest thing we have to a mystery-cult liturgy. It may be an Egyptian ritualist invoking a fashionable foreign god. The dispute is unresolved.

The material that is genuinely unpleasant

It would be dishonest to present this corpus as harmless antiquarian curiosity, because some of it is not.

A substantial group of texts are coercive attraction spells, and their language is brutal. They call on gods to deprive a named woman of sleep, food and rest until she comes to the operator. They specify her mother's name for identification. They are not requests for affection; they are requests for the destruction of someone's capacity to refuse.

These were not theoretical. A clay figurine now in the Louvre, pierced with thirteen bronze needles and buried in a jar alongside a lead tablet bearing precisely such a text, matches an operation in the papyri closely enough to establish that people carried out the full procedure exactly as written.

The historical value of this material is real, and so is what it reveals: much of ancient magic was ordinary people trying to exert control over other ordinary people who had not consented. Sanitising that would misrepresent the sources.

Why we have these and nothing else

The papyri present a puzzle when set beside everything else on this site. The mystery cults kept their secrets for two thousand years. These practitioners wrote everything down.

The difference is one of function rather than discipline. A mystery initiation was an event that happened to you once, in a hall, in a crowd, and its power depended on being undergone. A magical operation was a procedure that had to be executed correctly, in the right order, with the right words, possibly years after it was learned. Procedures need documentation. Experiences do not.

There is also the matter of what happened to such books elsewhere. Owning them was hazardous — magic was prosecutable under Roman law, Apuleius stood trial on exactly that charge, and later imperial and Christian authorities burned magical texts as policy. Egypt gave us a corpus not because Egyptians were more magical than anyone else, but because sand preserves papyrus, and because these particular rolls were buried before anyone could burn them.

Which means the honest description of the PGM is not “the magical literature of the ancient world.” It is the surviving fraction — the part that happened to end up somewhere dry.
