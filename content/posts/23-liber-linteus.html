In 1848 a Croatian civil servant named Mihajlo Barić quit his job in the Hungarian Royal Chancellery and went travelling. In Alexandria he bought a souvenir: a sarcophagus with a mummy inside it.

He took it home to Vienna and stood it upright in the corner of his sitting room. At some point the linen wrappings came off, and he had those displayed separately in a glass case — because they had writing on them, and writing was interesting.

He never found out what it said. Neither did anyone else for another forty years.

The woman in the case

Start with her, because she gets forgotten in the retelling.

A papyrus buried with the body identifies her as Nesi-hensu, the wife of Paher-hensu, a tailor from Thebes. She was Egyptian. She was wrapped, embalmed and buried according to Egyptian practice, by people doing an ordinary and careful job.

This matters because the popular version of the story tends to drift towards something more romantic — an Etruscan woman, an exiled family, a book buried with its owner. There's no evidence for any of that. What we have is an Egyptian burial that used second-hand cloth.

Linen was expensive. Mummification consumed enormous quantities of it, and reusing old textile was completely normal. Somewhere in Ptolemaic Egypt a roll of foreign linen with writing on it was available, and an embalmer's workshop bought it, cut it into strips, and used it.

How an Etruscan liturgical book got to Egypt in the first place is genuinely unknown. Etruscans travelled, traded, and served as mercenaries; Alexandria was full of everyone. Any answer beyond that is invention.

Forty years of nobody noticing

Barić died in 1859. His brother Ilija, a priest, inherited the mummy, had no use for it, and in 1867 gave it to the state institute in Zagreb, where it remains.

The writing was assumed to be Egyptian. That is not as stupid as it sounds — it was on an Egyptian mummy, in a museum full of Egyptian material, and the strips had been separated from each other, so nobody was looking at continuous text. Experts examined it and concluded it was hieratic, or possibly something else Egyptian, and moved on.

In 1891 Jacob Krall took the strips seriously. He worked out how they fitted together — which strip had been wound next to which, and therefore what order the text ran in — and once the columns were reassembled it stopped looking like decorated bandage and started looking like a page.

The language was Etruscan.

It was, at a stroke, the longest Etruscan text known, and it still is. Around 1,200 readable words across twelve columns. Every other substantial Etruscan document we have is shorter, and most of the corpus is one line long.

The only linen book left

There's a second thing about it that is easy to skate past. It is the only book written on linen to survive from the ancient world.

We know linen books existed. Livy refers to Roman libri lintei kept in the temple of Juno Moneta, recording lists of magistrates — and he treats them as a familiar kind of object, not a curiosity. Etruscan art shows figures holding what look like folded linen texts. The format was in use.

None of the others survived, because linen doesn't, unless something unusual happens to it. What happened here is that it was cut up, soaked in resin, and sealed inside a dry tomb around a body.

The book survived because somebody destroyed it as a book.

That is a pattern worth sitting with, because it isn't a one-off. Ancient texts don't usually reach us through libraries. Libraries need continuous upkeep and continuous copying, and the moment either stops, the collection is on a timer. What reaches us instead tends to be material that got buried, mislaid, recycled, or thrown out — text that was saved by accident, usually after somebody decided it wasn't worth reading.

What it says, and what that means

The content is a ritual calendar.

The structure is legible even where the meaning is not. Dates recur in a regular pattern — a month, a day. Deity names appear, some familiar from elsewhere. Verbs repeat in the same slots, in what are clearly instructions: make this offering, at this place, with this. Quantities appear. The phrasing is formulaic, in the way liturgical instructions always are.

So we can say with confidence what kind of document it is. It's a working handbook for performing ceremonies on the right days — the sort of thing a priest would consult, not something a believer would read.

What we cannot do is say what the ceremonies were for.

The problem isn't the script. Etruscan is written in an alphabet borrowed from Greek and can be transliterated with near-total confidence; scholars can pronounce the Liber Linteus. The problem is that Etruscan has no close relatives to translate from, so vocabulary has to be prised out of context, and a calendar of unexplained rites is exactly the sort of context that gives very little away.

It's the same wall the bronze liver from Piacenza runs into. We have the instrument and the schedule for a religion Rome took seriously enough to institutionalise for eight centuries, and we can describe the shape of both without knowing what either was actually saying.

The mummy and the book are both in Zagreb now, in a climate-controlled room. She is still unnamed in most accounts of the discovery, and the writing she was buried in is the longest surviving sentence of a language nobody has spoken for two thousand years — preserved because it was worth more as cloth than as literature.
