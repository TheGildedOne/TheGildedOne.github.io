As she went down the steps, her dress caught on something. Cornelia turned and freed it, and drew it back around her.

The executioner held out his hand to help her down. She pulled away from him, Pliny says, as if he were something filthy. She was the senior Vestal Virgin of Rome, and she was being buried alive.

All the way there she had kept calling on Vesta and the other gods. One line Pliny remembered: how could Caesar think her unchaste, when he had won his victories and his triumphs while she was making the sacrifices?

The room by the Colline Gate

The procedure was old, and Plutarch describes it in his Life of Numa. Inside the city wall near the Colline Gate, along an earth rampart, workmen dug a small room underground, with steps down into it. In it they put a couch with bedding, a lit lamp, and small amounts of bread, water, milk and oil.

The condemned Vestal was tied into a closed litter, bound so tightly that no cry could be heard from it, and carried through the Forum. People stood aside in silence. Plutarch says no sight in Rome was more appalling, and no day gloomier. At the rampart the cords were untied. The chief priest raised his hands to the sky and prayed, and led her veiled to the steps. Then he and the other priests turned their backs. When she was down, the steps were drawn up and the hole was filled in level with the ground.

The food is the giveaway. A Vestal's blood could not be shed, and the city could not be seen to kill her. With a lamp and a loaf she had not been starved to death. She had been handed back to the gods below, and what they did with her was their business.

Her lover, if one could be found, got no such legal tact. He was flogged to death in public.

The case against Cornelia

Cornelia was condemned around 90 CE by Domitian, who as emperor was also chief priest. He didn't call the priests to the Regia in the Forum, Pliny says, but to his villa at Alba. She was sentenced in her absence, without a hearing. Suetonius adds that she had been tried and acquitted once before, years earlier.

Pliny is careful about her guilt, and he's careful for a reason. He doesn't know. He says she seemed innocent, whether she was or not. The man accused with her, a knight called Celer, kept shouting under the rods, "What have I done? I've done nothing." A former praetor, Valerius Licinianus, confessed to hiding one of her freedwomen and was let off with exile, and Pliny suspects he confessed because confessing was safer than fighting.

Pliny wrote under Trajan, after Domitian's murder, when saying the worst about Domitian was not just safe but fashionable. So he isn't neutral either. What his letter does show is how little evidence the system needed. Livy says the Vestal Minucia, in 337 BCE, first fell under suspicion because she dressed more elegantly than was proper. A slave's testimony did the rest. Livy says the field where she was buried was called the Campus Sceleratus, the Wicked Field, because of her.

Bad news and bad Vestals

Here's the pattern that makes historians uneasy. Vestal trials are rare, and they tend to come right after something terrible has happened.

In 216 BCE Hannibal destroyed a Roman army at Cannae. Livy reports that in the same year two Vestals, Opimia and Floronia, were convicted of unchastity. One was buried by the Colline Gate. The other killed herself first. The clerk of the pontiffs accused with Floronia was beaten to death by the chief priest. Then the Sibylline Books were consulted, and on their advice a Gaulish man and woman and a Greek man and woman were buried alive in a stone chamber in the Forum Boarium.

In 114 BCE a girl called Helvia was struck by lightning while riding, a terrifying omen. Within two years three Vestals had been tried. The priests acquitted two of them, and a special court was set up to try them again and condemned them. Plutarch says that afterwards, again, a Greek and a Gaulish pair were buried alive.

So were these trials about sex at all? Mary Beard pointed out that the Vestals didn't fit any normal category: they were virgins, but dressed partly like brides and matrons, and held some rights only men had. Holt Parker went further. A Vestal's untouched body, he argued, stood for Rome itself, sealed and safe. When disaster came, Romans looked for the breach, and they knew where to look first.

That doesn't mean every accused Vestal was innocent. Some may well have taken lovers, and we can't tell which. It means the accusation had a second job, and when Rome needed someone to blame, the evidence didn't have to be good.

The fire and the statue

The Vestals kept the sacred fire burning in the round temple of Vesta, and guarded the objects stored inside it, which nobody else was allowed to see. One of them, Romans believed, was the Palladium, the image of Athena brought from Troy. The city's safety was locked in that building, and the women who tended it were part of the lock.

If the fire went out, the Vestal on duty was whipped by the chief priest, in the dark, behind a curtain. If her chastity was in doubt, she went to the Colline Gate.

Nobody has ever found the chamber. Somewhere under the modern streets of the Quirinal, presumably, there is a small room with a couch in it, and a lamp.
