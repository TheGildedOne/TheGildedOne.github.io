The man goes up the outside of the stone with a small chain looped round himself and the column, using pegs of wood as footholds, the way men climb palm trees in Arabia and Egypt. At the top he lets down a longer chain and hauls up wood, clothes and pots, and sits on them like a bird on its nest. He stays there for seven days.

Below him, people bring gold, silver and bronze. An attendant shouts out each giver's name, and the man at the top prays for them, striking a bronze instrument that clangs as he does it. If he falls asleep, they say, a scorpion climbs up and stings him awake.

The column he's sitting on is a phallus, one of a pair standing at the gates of the temple of the Syrian goddess at Hierapolis. According to the man telling us this, it's thirty fathoms high. That would be over fifty metres.

He's the only person in antiquity who mentions any of it.

An Assyrian in Herodotus' clothes

The book is called On the Syrian Goddess, a short description of the temple at Hierapolis, the "holy city", which the Greeks also called Bambyce and is now Manbij in northern Syria. It comes down to us among the works of Lucian of Samosata, the second-century satirist who also wrote the hatchet job on Alexander and his snake god Glycon.

It's an odd piece. It's written in Ionic Greek, the dialect of Herodotus, six centuries out of date, and it copies his manner: what I saw, what the priests told me, what I believe and what I don't. The narrator introduces himself as an Assyrian. He ends by telling us that as a young man he dedicated his own hair to the goddess, as the custom was, and it's still in the temple, with his name on it.

So which is it? A Syrian's sincere account of his own goddess, or a satirist's parody of a pious Herodotean tourist? For much of the twentieth century scholars either doubted Lucian wrote it or assumed it was a joke. Jaś Elsner read it as something subtler, a Greek-educated Syrian writing about his own culture in the language of an outsider. Jane Lightfoot, in her 2003 commentary, concluded that it's Lucian's, and that being Lucian's doesn't make it unreliable.

That matters, because for most of what it describes there is no second witness.

Lions, bulls and a golden sign

Lucian never calls the goddess Atargatis. He calls her Hera, and her consort Zeus, and says the locals had another name for him. Other writers fill it in: Pliny says Bambyce was where "the monstrous Atargatis" was worshipped, called Derceto by the Greeks. Her partner was the storm god Hadad.

Inside, Lucian says, sat two golden statues. Hers rested on lions, his on bulls. Between them stood a golden object with no shape of its own, topped by a golden dove, which even the Syrians had no settled name for. They just called it "the sign".

Here, for once, there's a check. Far down the Euphrates, at Dura-Europos, excavators around 1930 found a small limestone relief in the temple of Atargatis. The goddess sits between lions. Hadad is beside her with his bull. Between them is a standard. It is Lucian's arrangement, carved by someone who'd never read him.

The fish Zeus mustn't see

Beside the temple was a lake full of sacred fish. Some were huge and had names, Lucian says, and they came when called. One wore a gold ornament on its fin. The Syrians wouldn't eat them.

That at least is old. Xenophon, marching through Syria in 401 BCE, passed a river full of large tame fish that the Syrians regarded as gods and let nobody harm. Diodorus says that at Ascalon on the coast the goddess Derceto had a woman's face and a fish's body. Lucian says he saw her like that in Phoenicia, but at Hierapolis she was a woman all the way down.

At festivals the gods' images were carried down to the lake. Hera went first, Lucian was told, to keep Zeus from seeing the fish, because if he did, everyone would die. It's the kind of thing a priest tells a visitor, and the kind of thing a satirist enjoys writing down. Both can be true.

The festival of the knife

The great feast came in spring. Whole trees were set up in the courtyard and hung with live animals, clothes and gold, and the gods were carried around them before the lot was set on fire.

Then came the galli, the goddess's eunuch priests, gashing their arms while flutes and drums played. Some of the young men watching would be caught up in it. Lucian says one would strip, seize one of the swords that had been kept ready there for years, Lucian supposes, castrate himself and run through the city holding what he'd cut off. He'd throw it into a house, and that house had to give him women's clothes.

Readers will know the scene from Cybele's galli in Rome, and the Romans didn't always tell the two goddesses apart. Apuleius, in the same novel that ends with the mysteries of Isis, has a troupe of the Syrian goddess's priests tramping the villages of Greece with her statue on a donkey, whipping themselves for coins and cheating everyone. The donkey is the narrator. Suetonius says Nero despised every cult except the Syrian goddess's, then turned on her too, and urinated on her image.

What we can't check

The phallus climber has no other witness. Neither does the castration in the street, the scorpion, the golden-finned fish, or the story of Combabus, the courtier who castrated himself rather than be suspected with the queen and became the galli's founder. We don't know how much Lucian saw, how much the priests told him, and how much he improved.

He says he did see one thing himself. The bearded Apollo whose image chose its own direction when the priests carried it once left them standing on the ground, he says, and rose into the air alone.
