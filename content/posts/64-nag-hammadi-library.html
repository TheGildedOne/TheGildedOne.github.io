The jar was red earthenware, buried by a boulder at the foot of a cliff.

Muhammad 'Ali al-Samman and his brothers had come out to the Jabal al-Tarif, across the Nile from Nag Hammadi, to dig for sabakh, the nitrate-rich soil farmers used as fertiliser. It was December 1945. When his mattock hit the jar, he hesitated. A jinn might live in it. Then again, it might be full of gold.

He smashed it. There was no gold. Scraps of papyrus flew out, and inside were books: a stack of them, bound in leather like modern ones.

The story everyone tells

What came next is the most retold discovery story in early Christian studies.

Muhammad 'Ali took the books home to al-Qasr and dumped them on the straw by the oven. His mother, Umm-Ahmad, later admitted she'd burned some of them to light the fire. A few weeks later he and his brothers tracked down Ahmad Isma'il, the man they believed had killed their father, and murdered him. In his telling, they hacked out the man's heart and ate it.

With the police likely to search the house, he left a book with the local priest. A history teacher, Raghib Andrawus, saw it, guessed it was valuable, and sent it to Cairo. Dealers got involved. One codex reached the Coptic Museum in October 1946. Most of another was smuggled out, bought in the early 1950s by the Jung Institute in Zurich as a present for Carl Jung himself, and only returned to Cairo in the 1970s.

Elaine Pagels opened The Gnostic Gospels with this version in 1979, and it's hard to blame her. It's a fantastic story.

One witness, thirty years on

It's also mostly one man's account. The jar, the jinn, the gold, the oven and the heart all come from interviews that James M. Robinson, who led the international project to publish the codices, held with Muhammad 'Ali in the 1970s. That's three decades after the fact, and by then the story had been told many times.

In 2013 Mark Goodacre went back over the versions. The earliest, from the French scholar Jean Doresse, was gathered about five years after the find, and it differs from Robinson's in important ways. Doresse was taken to the edge of an ancient cemetery and told the books had come from there. Robinson's puts them under a boulder at the cliff. Goodacre's conclusion was blunt: the early, dull version is more reliable than the later, vivid one. Muhammad 'Ali himself gave different answers at different times about whether the books were in a jar, in a cave, or beside a body.

Even the number is shaky. The find is always called thirteen codices. There are twelve, plus eight leaves of a thirteenth tucked inside the cover of Codex VI. Robinson thought the pages that went into Umm-Ahmad's oven came from Codex XII, which survives only in scraps. We can't check.

What was in them

None of this changes what came out of the ground, and that was astonishing. Fifty-two texts in Coptic, most of them unknown. The only complete copy of the Gospel of Thomas. The Gospel of Philip, the Apocryphon of John, the Gospel of Truth. Three Hermetic treatises. And, oddly, a few pages of Plato's Republic in a rough Coptic translation.

Many of these books had been known only through the people attacking them. Church writers like Irenaeus had described "Gnostic" teachings in order to refute them, and the texts themselves had vanished. It's the same pattern as the prophetic books Augustus burned: we know they existed mostly because someone recorded getting rid of them.

Who buried them

This is the bit that's really argued over.

The leather covers were stiffened with waste papyrus, old letters and receipts glued together. When these were peeled apart and published in 1981, the cover of Codex VII turned out to contain a document dated to 348 CE, and letters mentioning a Pachome and a Papnoute. The great monastery founded by Pachomius, the father of communal monasticism, stood a few miles away.

Robinson put the pieces together into a tidy story. In 367 Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, sent his annual Easter letter round Egypt's churches listing the books Christians should read, and warning against apocryphal ones. The Pachomian monks, he suggested, hid their heretical library in a jar rather than destroy it.

It's a nice idea. Nothing in the jar says so.

Nicola Denzey Lewis and Justine Ariel Blount, in 2014, rejected the monastery altogether. The books, they argued, were more likely private copies made for wealthy Christians with a taste for unusual texts, and the find-spot by a cemetery points to a burial with the dead. That isn't as strange as it sounds. Books did go into ancient graves: the Derveni Papyrus survived only because it went onto a funeral pyre.

Hugo Lundhaug and Lance Jenott went the other way in 2015. They argued that Pachomian monks did make and read the codices, but not as secret heretics. Fourth-century monks read widely, and the idea of a sharp line between orthodox and heretical shelves is ours more than theirs. Critics point out that the waste paper in the covers could simply have been bought by the bundle, and tells us who made the bindings, not who read the books.

Which leaves us roughly where Muhammad 'Ali did. A jar, a boulder, and no label. As at Alexandria, the real story is less a dramatic destruction than a slow loss nobody bothered to record.

One object is supposed to have come out of the ground with the books: a pottery bowl, laid in the jar's mouth as a lid. Robinson bought it in 1976 from a household in al-Qasr that had kept it as a talisman. Its link to the find rests on Muhammad 'Ali's brother Khalifah, who Muhammad 'Ali said wasn't even there.

In 2024 Christie's offered it for sale, in one lot with the leather cover of Codex I.
