At Housesteads, on Hadrian's Wall, there is a small stone altar that has nothing obviously to do with Britain. It was set up by the First Cohort of Tungrians, soldiers recruited from what is now Belgium, garrisoned on the coldest edge of the Roman Empire. The dedication on it says the offering was made "according to the interpretation of the oracle of Clarian Apollo." The oracle in question sat on the coast of Ionia, more than two thousand miles away, in a temple most of those soldiers would never see.

A temple with a basement

Claros, also written Klaros, stood near the city of Colophon on the western coast of Anatolia, a short distance inland from the sea. Like Delphi, it was sacred to Apollo, and like Delphi it drew delegations from cities across the Greek and later the Roman world asking questions on the state's behalf: whether to found a colony, how to survive a plague, what a strange omen meant. Unlike Delphi, where the Pythia sat inside the temple itself, the mechanism at Claros ran underground.

Beneath the temple floor, excavation has uncovered a vaulted chamber reached by two narrow corridors, cut low enough that anyone moving through them had to stoop. Inside sat a spring, its water reputed to carry the god's inspiration. Delegations arrived and submitted their questions during the day. The actual consultation happened after dark.

What happened in the dark

Ancient accounts describe a small team working the ritual rather than a single figure alone. A prophet, a local man appointed to the role and, by most descriptions, not himself trained as a poet, went down with a thespode, a professional reciter, and by some reconstructions a secretary as well. The prophet fasted beforehand and drank from the underground spring. What followed, in the accounts that describe it at all, was first incoherent sound, the prophet in some altered state induced by fasting, darkness, and the water itself, and then verse: the thespode is generally understood to have shaped the prophet's utterance into the finished hexameter lines that delegations carried home and, often, carved onto public inscriptions back in their own cities.

How much of that process was spontaneous and how much prepared is exactly the part no ancient writer lays out plainly enough to settle. Robin Lane Fox, whose 1986 account of the empire's oracles remains one of the fuller modern treatments, reconstructs the scene from scattered testimony and the inscriptions themselves rather than from any single description of the ritual from start to finish, because no such description survives. We have the questions, we have the answers, and we have fragments of the theatre in between. The mechanism connecting them is inference, the same gap that has kept modern scholars arguing over what actually put the Pythia at Delphi into her trance.

A wall of questions

What does survive, in unusual quantity, is the paper trail. Hundreds of inscriptions from cities across Asia Minor and beyond record Claros oracles verbatim, carved into walls, steps, and a curved marble bench at the sanctuary itself: the largest surviving body of oracular text from anywhere in Greek antiquity, on a different scale from the thousands of scrap lead questions left at Dodona, which recorded the asking rather than the answering. Cities asked what god to honour, how to end a plague, occasionally something closer to theology outright. One well-known oracle, requested by the city of Oenoanda in Lycia and then inscribed on its own city wall, answers a question about the nature of god with a hymn describing an invisible, self-generated fire beyond ordinary description, a text later writers, pagan and Christian both, found useful for arguments neither side entirely intended.

One answer, an empire wide

The clearest evidence of how far a single Claros answer could travel comes from the 160s CE, during the Antonine plague that spread through the Roman world under Marcus Aurelius. An oracle issued from Claros, apparently recommending a specific ritual response, was copied onto dedications from Italy to the Balkans to, as the Housesteads altar shows, a fort on the northern edge of Britain. At least ten comparable inscriptions survive across the empire, all traceable to the same plague-era response. A question asked in Colophon produced an answer that soldiers on Hadrian's Wall were still acting on when they set stone into a wall of their own.

The sanctuary itself was identified in 1907 and properly excavated in stages across the twentieth century, first under Louis Robert from the 1950s, who uncovered the underground chamber and much of the inscription corpus, then under Juliette de La Genière from 1988 to 1997, whose work traced the site's archaic origins further back than Robert had reached. Between them they recovered the stage the ritual was performed on. What actually happened, night after night, in the low stone corridors beneath the temple, stays mostly where it always was: in the verses, and not in any account of how the verses got made.
