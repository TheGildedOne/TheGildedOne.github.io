"Am I going to be sold?"

It sits in a numbered list, a few lines away from "Will I inherit from my father?" and "Will I sail safely?" Somebody in Roman Egypt needed that question often enough that it made the cut: one of ninety-two things a person might want to ask the gods, set down in advance, with ten possible answers waiting for each.

The book it comes from is called the Sortes Astrampsychi, the Lots of Astrampsychus. It is a fortune-teller's manual, and it still works. You could consult it this afternoon.

How the trick works

You pick your question and note its number. Then you choose a number from one to ten. The idea is that this isn't really your choice: the god puts the number in your mind. You add the two together.

Now you look the total up in a table. It sends you to a "decade," a block of ten answers headed by the name of a god. You count down to the answer matching your chosen number, and there's your reply.

Here's the clever bit. The ten possible answers to any one question are not kept together. They are scattered across ten different decades, mixed in with answers to completely unrelated questions. There are 103 decades, which makes 1,030 answers, and some of them answer nothing at all: dummies, there to fill the gaps. Leaf through the book and you'd never work out the system. It just looks like a thousand pronouncements from a hundred gods.

The book even has rules about timing. On seven days of each month you could not consult it at all, and on some others only in the morning or the afternoon.

Will I be freed?

The questions are the best thing in it. Will I be able to borrow the money? Will I open a workshop? Will I pay back what I owe? Am I safe from prosecution? Will I beat my opponent in court? Will the traveller come home? Will I be freed from slavery?

It's the same private anxiety that fills the lead question strips at Dodona, several centuries on and a long way south. Except that here somebody has done the anxieties in advance and numbered them.

The answers range from blunt to barbed. Ask whether you'll get the woman you want, and one possible reply is that yes, you will, very much to your cost. Ask about an estranged wife, and you might hear that you'll be reconciled and sorry for it.

Franziska Naether, who has studied the book more closely than anyone, sorts the questions by subject: about a fifth are about property and inheritance, and large shares go to public office, business, and love and marriage. From that she reconstructs the typical user as a man, married, middle-aged, reasonably well off, often travelling and holding local office. Which makes "Am I going to be sold?" all the more interesting. Somebody much further down Egyptian society was expected to use this book too, or at least to have questions asked on their behalf.

Pythagoras, Ptolemy and a borrowed magician

The book opens with a covering letter from "Astrampsychus the Egyptian" to "King Ptolemy," explaining that the method was invented by Pythagoras, and that Alexander the Great ruled the world by using it. None of this is true. Astrampsychus was a name borrowed from a legendary magician, and the whole preface is packaging, the ancient equivalent of a celebrity endorsement on a self-help book.

Magic in Roman Egypt liked this kind of pedigree. The spellbooks in the Greek Magical Papyri are full of recipes credited to famous sages who never saw them. The real author of the Sortes is anonymous, and was probably working in the second or third century CE.

Who held the book?

This is the part still argued over. All that shuffling makes most sense if the client never saw the book. Picture a professional at a table: you tell him your question and your number, he consults the volume, and reads out the god's reply. The system's opacity is part of the product. You can't check the working, so it feels like a god answered.

But nothing in the text requires a specialist either. Some lot oracles only worked with a copy of Homer to hand. The Sortes is self-contained: book, question, number, answer. Anyone literate who owned a copy could run it at home.

We don't know which. Around a dozen papyrus copies survive, from the third century onwards, several from the rubbish mounds of Oxyrhynchus. None of them comes with a picture of who was holding it. A dice oracle carved on a pillar at least tells you it was public. A book on papyrus could be in anybody's hands.

From Zeus to the saints

The book didn't die with the old gods. It was far too useful. Christian users kept the machinery and swapped the pagan names in the answer tables for biblical ones. They also rewrote some of the questions for a world with a church hierarchy in it.

The complete versions survive only in Byzantine manuscripts copied between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries, long after Egypt's temples had closed, all of them adapted this way. David Ratzan, comparing the earlier and later forms, found the earlier answers noticeably more confident about money. And somewhere along the line, the question "Will I be reconciled with my girlfriend?" was quietly crossed out and replaced. It now reads: "Will I become a bishop?"
