Ancient literature was written by a very narrow group of people. Aristocrats, more or less, with the leisure to compose and the resources to have copies made. When we say we know what the Greeks and Romans thought, we mostly mean we know what a few thousand wealthy men wrote down.

Curse tablets are the exception, and it is a large one. Over sixteen hundred of them survive, scratched into lead by people who were often barely literate, and they were never meant to be read by anyone alive. They contain no rhetoric, no self-presentation, no audience management. Just a name, a wish, and instructions.

They are, in effect, sixteen hundred pieces of ancient mail addressed to the underworld — and they were undeliverable, so we still have them.

The procedure

The method is remarkably consistent across eight centuries and the whole Mediterranean, which tells us something about how such knowledge circulated.

Take a thin sheet of lead, often hammered out from scrap or cut from a water pipe. Scratch the text with a stylus or a nail. Then roll or fold the sheet, sometimes pierce it through with a nail, and deposit it where the chthonic powers can receive it.

Deposition sites are where the logic shows. Graves are the most common — and preferentially the graves of the aoroi and biaiothanatoi, those who died before their time or by violence. The reasoning was that such spirits had not settled, remained near their remains, and were therefore both present and aggrieved. A restless ghost was an available intermediary.

Wells and springs served the same purpose as openings downward. So did the sanctuaries of underworld deities. In one exceptional case at Bath, they went into hot water rising out of the earth, which apparently counted as a channel running the other way.

Many tablets manipulate the writing itself. Text runs right to left, or in a spiral, or letters are reversed, or the name of the victim is written backwards. This is sympathetic logic made typographic: distort the writing, distort the person.

What people actually asked for

The functional categories are clear enough that they have become standard, and the pattern is worth noticing before the details.

Lawsuits. One of the largest groups. Overwhelmingly the request is not that the opponent lose — it is that they be unable to speak. Bind his tongue. Freeze his mind. Let him forget his arguments before the jury. In a legal culture where cases were decided by oral performance in front of large juries, the ability to speak was the case.

Competition. Athletes cursed rivals. Actors cursed other actors. And a whole industry existed around the circus — tablets from Carthage and Rome curse named charioteers and their horses, listing the animals individually, asking that they crash, stumble, or fail to leave the gate.

Business. Tavern keepers, shopkeepers, workshop owners cursing the competitor down the street. Bind his hands, his trade, his customers.

Love, in the worst sense. Two kinds. Separation curses, aiming to break an existing couple apart. And attraction curses, which are the ugliest material in the corpus — requests that a named person be denied sleep, food and peace until they come to the operator. As with the equivalent recipes in the magical papyri, these are not about affection. They are about overriding refusal.

The pattern underneath the list

Nearly every category is agonistic — it concerns a contest with a specific rival, in a society with limited goods and public reputation at stake. These are not attempts at general malice or world-shaping power. They are attempts to win a particular fight against a particular named person, by someone who evidently expected to lose without help.

Bath: the tablets that are not curses

Around 130 tablets were recovered from the sacred spring at Aquae Sulis, modern Bath, thrown into the hot water that rises there. They form a distinct genre, and reading them changes the impression the corpus otherwise gives.

The overwhelming subject is theft — and specifically theft at the baths, where people had to leave their clothes unattended while bathing. Someone's cloak has been taken. Someone's gloves. Six coins. A bath tunic. A ploughshare.

The formula is not vengeance so much as legal appeal. The writer tells the goddess Sulis Minerva what was taken, frequently states that they are giving the stolen property to her — transferring ownership to the deity, which reframes the theft as an offence against a god rather than a person — and asks her to afflict the thief until the item is returned to the temple.

Because the thief is unknown, the tablets often list suspects by name, or cover the possibility comprehensively: whether man or woman, slave or free, boy or girl. One writer asks that the culprit lose their mind and their eyes in the goddess's temple. Another asks that the thief become as liquid as the water — which, given where the tablet was thrown, is a rather good line.

Scholars call these prayers for justice rather than curses, and the distinction is real. The writer has been wronged, is powerless to identify the culprit, has no realistic legal recourse for a stolen cloak, and appeals to the one authority available. This is not sorcery. It is what people did when there was no one else to complain to.

Why they matter more than they look

Three things emerge from this corpus that we could not get any other way.

Ordinary literacy was more widespread than assumed. Many tablets are ungrammatical, misspelt, or written in shaky letters — and that is the point. Some were clearly commissioned from professionals, since near-identical formulae recur in the same hand across multiple tablets. But many are homemade, by people writing badly and writing anyway.

The magic/religion boundary does not hold. The Bath tablets address a goddess in her own temple using the language of petition, and are formally indistinguishable from prayer. Elsewhere the same technology is used for straightforward malice. The ancient distinction was not between religion and magic as categories, but between practices the community endorsed and practices it did not — a social judgement, applied inconsistently.

We are reading real grievances. A stolen tunic. A rival tavern. A court case that will decide whether a family keeps its property. A charioteer who keeps winning. These are the concerns of people whose names appear nowhere else in the historical record, and who exist for us solely because they were angry enough to write it in lead and bury it.

Plato complains in the Republic about itinerant specialists who go door to door offering binding spells for hire. He means it dismissively. What he is actually recording is a functioning service industry, meeting steady demand, across the whole ancient world — and its customer records happen to be made of a metal that does not rot.
