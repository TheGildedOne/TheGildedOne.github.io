The baby is wrapped in its swaddling clothes, and the man holding it has a knife at its throat.

He's an old man in a dress, and he has just been unmasked. Around him are the women of Athens, furious. He's grabbed a child from one of them, and he's threatening to kill it at the altar unless they let him go. Then he unwraps it. The baby is a wineskin, in little shoes.

That's Aristophanes, in his comedy Women at the Thesmophoria, staged in 411 BCE. The joke works because every man in the audience knew there was a festival his wife went to every autumn, and that he would never be allowed to see it.

Three days without men

The Thesmophoria was a festival of Demeter, the grain goddess, and her daughter Persephone. It was held in towns all over the Greek world, and in Athens it ran for three days in Pyanepsion, around October, when the seed went into the ground.

It was for married citizen women. They left their houses, set up shelters together at the sanctuary, and stayed there. Their husbands paid. The orator Isaeus mentions a man obliged, as a husband, to give a feast for the women of his district at the festival. He uses it as evidence in a lawsuit over who was legally married to whom. To have a wife at the Thesmophoria was to have a wife.

The first day was the anodos, the ascent. The second was a fast. The women sat on mats of plants that were supposed to dampen desire, and mourned like Demeter mourning her stolen daughter. Aristophanes sets his play on this middle day. The third was for Kalligeneia, the goddess of fair birth.

That much is fairly solid. Then there are the pigs.

What came up from the pits

The famous part of the festival is this. Piglets were thrown, alive or dead, into pits called megara, along with dough shaped like snakes and like male genitals, and pine branches. Later, women called "bailers", who had kept themselves pure for three days, climbed down into the pits and brought up what was left. Snakes lived down there, it was said, and ate most of it, so the women clapped their hands to frighten them off. The rotted remains were laid on the altars. Anyone who took some and mixed it with their seed would get a good harvest.

Why pigs? In myth, when the earth opened to swallow Persephone, a swineherd called Eubouleus was grazing his herd on that spot, and the pigs went down into the chasm with her. In practice, our source says, because pigs breed so fast.

Walter Burkert called it about the clearest case of agrarian magic in Greek religion, and you'll find it retold in every book on the subject. So it's worth knowing where it comes from. Not from any classical Athenian. It comes from a scholion: an anonymous note written in the margin of medieval manuscripts of Lucian, explaining a passing reference to the festival in one of his Dialogues of the Courtesans. We don't know who wrote it, or when, or what he was copying.

He was clearly copying something, and it may be good. But the note is muddled. Its own editors have found parts of it obscure, and it isn't even clear whether the piglets went into the pits at the Thesmophoria or months earlier at another festival. It offers a myth and a natural explanation side by side, which reads more like a scholar explaining a custom than a woman remembering one. Nobody who took part ever wrote anything down.

The archaeology helps a little. Sanctuaries of Demeter in Sicily have produced pits with pig bones in them, and Demeter's shrines from Gela to Corinth are full of clay figurines of women carrying piglets. The pig was hers. The pit, the snakes and the bailers we have on the word of one note.

Keeping men out

Herodotus knew more, and wouldn't say. He claims the rite came from Egypt with the daughters of Danaus, and then adds that he'll keep silent about it, beyond what is lawful to tell. That's the same reticence ancient writers kept about Eleusis, the other great secret of Demeter's worship, and it worked just as well.

The stories about men who broke in are all violent. Pausanias tells how Aristomenes, a hero of the Messenian wars against Sparta, tried to seize women celebrating a festival of Demeter at Aigila. The women fought him with sacrificial knives, roasting spits and torches, and took him prisoner. The Roman version of the scandal ended in a trial, when Clodius was caught in women's clothes at the Bona Dea's night rite in 62 BCE.

Aristophanes turned it into farce, but the fear is the same one. Euripides, in the play, sends his relative in disguise because he's heard the women plan to use their festival to decide his punishment, for writing such nasty things about them. The joke is that the women, left alone, hold an assembly, with motions and speeches, exactly like the men.

A festival with no address

Where did the Athenian women actually go? For a long time the answer was the Pnyx, the hill where the men's assembly met, which would make Aristophanes' joke even neater. In 1942 Oscar Broneer, digging just south of the hill, pointed out that the whole idea rested on a single line in the play, which needn't mean that at all.

The Thesmophorion of Athens has never been securely found. Thousands of women spent three days there every year for centuries, and we don't know which hill it was on.

If the note in the margin is right, what they brought up from the pits went out to the fields, mixed into the seed corn, and was ploughed in.
