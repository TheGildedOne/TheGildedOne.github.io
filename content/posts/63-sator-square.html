In November 1936 Matteo Della Corte was working through the Large Palaestra at Pompeii, the big exercise ground beside the amphitheatre, reading the graffiti on its columns.

Pompeii's walls are full of graffiti. Election slogans, insults, bills, boasts about girlfriends. On one column he found something tidier: five words cut neatly into the plaster, one above the other.

ROTAS
OPERA
TENET
AREPO
SATOR

Read it across, down, backwards or upwards. It says the same thing every way. It's a perfect Latin palindrome, and it had been sitting under the ash since 79 CE.

The square that went everywhere

Della Corte knew exactly what he was looking at, because the square was already famous. Eleven years earlier he'd found a fragment of one in the house of Paquius Proculus, a few streets away, and not realised what it was until later.

It turns up across the Roman world. Excavators at Dura-Europos, on the Euphrates, found four on the walls of a military building from around 200 CE. One was scratched on a plaster wall at Cirencester in Roman Britain, found in 1868. Another was on a fragment of pottery dug up in Manchester in 1978, from the late second century.

Then it outlived Rome entirely. A Coptic papyrus in Berlin carries it. An Ethiopian Christian prayer-scroll, the Bandlet of Righteousness, gives the five nails of the cross as Sador, Alador, Danet, Adera and Rodas, which is the square in a slightly crumpled form. Medieval builders carved it into churches in Italy and France. It kept its power long after anyone could say what it meant.

Which is the problem. Nobody can.

What it says, roughly

Four of the words are ordinary Latin. SATOR is a sower. TENET means "holds". OPERA is "with effort" or "works". ROTAS is "wheels". The usual translation runs something like: "The sower Arepo holds the wheels with effort."

That leaves AREPO, which isn't a Latin word at all. Jérôme Carcopino thought it was Gaulish for a plough. J. Gwyn Griffiths derived it from an Egyptian name. Miroslav Marcovich read it as a shortened Harpocrates, the child Horus. Others make it a man's name. Duncan Fishwick's answer was the most deflating: it's the word you get when the other four are in place and the square has to close.

He's probably right. The point of the thing is the pattern, and the pattern doesn't care whether Arepo was a farmer.

The prayer inside it

In the mid-1920s three scholars, working separately, noticed something astonishing. Christian Frank published it in 1924, the German priest Felix Grosser in 1926, the Swede Sigurd Agrell in 1927.

Take the twenty-five letters and lay them out as a cross. They spell PATERNOSTER down and PATERNOSTER across, sharing the N in the middle. "Our Father", the first words of the Lord's Prayer, twice. And you have exactly four letters left over: two As and two Os. Put one of each at the end of every arm and you've got Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end, which is what Christ calls himself in the Book of Revelation.

The odds of that happening by accident are very long. For about a decade it looked solved. The square was a secret password of persecuted Christians, hiding their prayer in a word game.

Then Della Corte found it at Pompeii.

Too early for Christians

The palaestra square can't have been written after the eruption. Carcopino suggested that later treasure hunters tunnelled in and scratched it, but the deposits over the palaestra were undisturbed. The plaster it was cut into is usually dated before the earthquake of 62 CE.

So now the Christian reading has to explain a lot. Guillaume de Jerphanion, who had himself added to the Christian reading, set out the difficulties in 1937. Were there Christians at Pompeii at all? Maybe a few, but we have no good evidence. Early Christians wrote in Greek, not Latin. The cross isn't used as a Christian symbol in any source until well into the second century. Alpha and Omega come from Revelation, which hadn't been written yet. And hiding your faith in code makes sense in a persecution, which in Pompeii's lifetime had barely started.

None of that is fatal on its own. Together, it's a lot.

Jewish, Mithraic, or nobody's

The rival theories all start from the same awkward fact: whatever the PATERNOSTER cross is, the Pompeii square came first.

Fishwick, writing in the Harvard Theological Review in 1964, argued that it was made by Latin-speaking Jews in Campania. "Our Father" is a Jewish way to address God too. The T at the heart of the square might be the Hebrew tau, the mark of the saved in Ezekiel. Later Christians, he suggested, took over a Jewish charm and found their own meaning in it.

Walter Moeller went somewhere else entirely in 1973. He read it as a product of the cult of Mithras, pulling astronomical and sacred numbers out of the letters by converting them to Greek numerals. It hasn't won many converts. Enough arithmetic will pull meaningful numbers out of almost anything.

Donald Atkinson, in 1938, had put it somewhere vaguer and probably more honest: in the overlap where religion, superstition and magic meet. That's where the later squares clearly lived. Roman Egypt was full of letter-shapes with power in their form, like the vanishing word-triangles in the magical papyri and the strings of vowels carved on magical gems. A word that reads the same in every direction is hard to break. That's the logic of a charm.

Rose Mary Sheldon, who surveyed the whole debate in Cryptologia in 2003, gave her article a title ending in a question mark. It's still the right punctuation.

Back at the column

Here's what we actually have. A word square, already polished, at Pompeii before 79 CE. A chance anagram, or not, that Christians may later have recognised. And a word, AREPO, that has defeated everyone who has looked at it.

The column it was found on is Pompeii's number 61 in the palaestra. Scratched just below the square, by the same hand or another, are a name, Sautran, and a single word.

Vale. Goodbye.
