On Rhodes, Tiberius had a house on a cliff above the sea, and that's where he consulted astrologers.

The arrangement was simple. One freedman, strong and illiterate, led each astrologer up the rough path to the top. If Tiberius decided the man was a fraud, or just knew too much, the freedman threw him into the sea on the way down.

Thrasyllus passed the first part. He read Tiberius' future well, and promised him the empire. So Tiberius asked him to read his own stars. Thrasyllus did the sums, went pale, and said he was in danger of his life at that very moment. Tiberius embraced him and kept him for the rest of his life.

Tacitus tells the story (Annals 6.21), and admits he can't decide whether fate is real. Either way, the man who became emperor in 14 CE had an astrologer at his elbow. Two years later his senate threw the astrologers out of Italy.

The man who wanted to pave the Appian Way

The trigger was Marcus Scribonius Libo Drusus, a young aristocrat with a famous family and not much sense. A friend steered him towards astrologers, magicians and dream interpreters. When he was prosecuted in 16 CE, the evidence included a list of questions he'd put to his prophets. One asked whether he'd ever be rich enough to cover the Appian Way, all the way to Brundisium, with money.

That was silly. Others were not. One document set mysterious marks beside the names of the imperial family. A man named Junius said Libo had asked him to call up the dead with incantations. Libo killed himself before the verdict.

The senate then expelled the astrologers and magicians from Italy. Two were made examples of. Lucius Pituanius was thrown from the Tarpeian Rock. Publius Marcius was executed by the consuls outside the Esquiline Gate "in the ancient manner". Tacitus didn't explain the phrase. His readers didn't need him to.

It was the same reign that turned on the worshippers of Isis three years later. Foreign knowledge was easy to expel. Thrasyllus stayed.

Again, and again

It wasn't new. The first expulsion we hear of was in 139 BCE, when the praetor Cornelius Hispalus ordered the "Chaldaeans" out of Rome and Italy. We know about it only from two late abridgements of a lost book by Valerius Maximus, so the details are thin.

In 33 BCE Agrippa, as aedile, drove the astrologers and charlatans from the city. In 11 CE Augustus banned anyone from consulting a seer alone, or asking about a death, even with witnesses present. In the same breath he published his own horoscope by edict. Augustus also burned over two thousand prophetic books, and put his sign, Capricorn, on his coins.

In 52 CE, after Furius Scribonianus was exiled for asking astrologers when Claudius would die, the senate expelled them again. Tacitus calls the decree savage and useless.

In 69 Vitellius ordered them out of Italy by the first of October. A notice went up at once, signed by the Chaldaeans, announcing that Vitellius would be dead by the same date. He was killed that December. Late, but not by much.

Vespasian banned them too, while keeping his own. So did Domitian.

Always banned, always kept

Tacitus sums them up in a line of the Histories: a breed of men that in our city will always be forbidden, and always kept.

The logic isn't hard to see. A horoscope could tell you when the emperor would die. Whoever knew that knew when to move. So an emperor wanted the best astrologer in Rome working for him, and nobody else's astrologer asking the same question. Frederick Cramer's 1954 study, still the standard collection, traced how often astrology turned up in treason trials. Tamsyn Barton put it plainly: astrology was a kind of political knowledge.

Did the expulsions work? Tacitus didn't think so, and the fact that they keep repeating suggests he was right. Pauline Ripat started in 2011 from a more basic question: how did anyone actually pick out an astrologer to expel? It's a good one. There was no licence and no uniform. A man with a star chart and a good memory could call himself a philosopher, a doctor or a mathematician. Most bans seem to have followed a specific scare, swept up a few unlucky foreigners, and left the well-connected alone.

We don't know how many people ever left Italy because of one.

The hour

Domitian, says Suetonius, had known since his youth the year, the day and the hour he would die. It made him jumpy.

He put an astrologer called Ascletarion on trial and asked him how he himself would die. Torn apart by dogs, quite soon, Ascletarion said. Domitian had him executed and ordered a careful funeral, to prove the art was nonsense. A sudden storm knocked over the pyre, and dogs mauled the half-burned body.

On the last day of his life in 96 CE, Domitian asked the time. He'd been told to fear the fifth hour. It was the fifth hour. His servants told him it was the sixth. Relieved, he went off to bathe, and his chamberlain brought in a man with news of a plot, and a dagger. In Ephesus, if Cassius Dio is right, Apollonius of Tyana was shouting the assassin's name.
