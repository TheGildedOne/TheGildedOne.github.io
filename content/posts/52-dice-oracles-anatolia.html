A sheep's ankle bone is a small, knobbly, oddly satisfying thing. It fits in a closed fist. Thrown, it can't land on its rounded ends, so it settles on one of four sides: two broad, two narrow. Greeks gave those sides the values 1, 3, 4 and 6, and for most of antiquity these bones were what people meant by dice. Children played with them. Adults gambled with them. And in the market squares of Roman Anatolia, people used them to ask the gods what to do next.

A timetable of answers

Across the mountains of southern Turkey, in the old regions of Pisidia, Pamphylia and Lycia, tall stone pillars and wall blocks stood in public places, carved with columns of text. Each column is a list. Each entry starts with a throw of five knucklebones, spelled out, and its total. Then comes the name of a god. Then a line or two of verse: the god's reply.

Five bones with four scoring sides can land in exactly fifty-six combinations, and the pillars list all fifty-six. The lowest throw, five ones, is five points. The highest, five sixes, is thirty. Every combination in between has its own god and its own verse.

One reply, for a throw of three ones, a six and a four, tells you to sail wherever you like: you'll come home happy, having found everything you had in mind. Others are less cheerful. Some tell you to wait. A few warn you, more or less, to stop pushing the god for an answer he doesn't want to give.

The inscriptions cluster in the second and third centuries CE. The one at Kremna in Pisidia, which stood in the town's market square, dates to the reign of Hadrian. Others come from Perge, Termessos, Sagalassos, Adada and a scatter of smaller places. Isolated examples had been known for nearly two hundred years before Johannes Nollé gathered the whole group into a single edition in 2007.

The oracle with nobody in it

Think about what's missing. At Delphi you needed a Pythia, priests, a sacrifice, a fee, a queue and a good deal of patience. At Claros a prophet went underground at night on your behalf. Even the Roman state, taking the auspices, needed a keeper of sacred chickens and a trained augur to read what the birds did.

Here, you needed five bones and the ability to read. You could carry your own knucklebones. You threw, found your line on the pillar, and walked off into the market with the god's opinion on your deal.

There was a more hands-on version in mainland Greece. Pausanias describes a small statue of Heracles in a cave near Bura, in Achaea, where you prayed in front of the image, took four dice from a heap left beside it, and threw them on a table. Every possible throw had its meaning written on a board. He mentions no priest at all.

Many of the Anatolian replies address the reader as a stranger, a visitor with business to do. That, plus their obsession with journeys and deals, suggests merchants and travellers were a big part of the audience.

Budget oracle, or better one?

This is where the argument starts. Was this a cheap cousin of the great oracles, divination for people who couldn't afford Delphi? Or something people trusted more, precisely because nobody stood between them and the god?

The snobbish view has a good ancient pedigree. Cicero, writing in the 40s BCE, is scathing about lots in general. What is a lot, he asks, but the same thing as playing odds and evens, or throwing knucklebones and dice? He says the famous lot oracle at Praeneste was by then trusted only by the common people. A Roman senator who thought lots were a game was never going to be impressed by a stone full of them.

But the pillars themselves don't look cheap. They are big, carefully carved public monuments, standing in the busiest parts of town, and they come from exactly the century when the old oracles were booming again. Nollé's title for them places them in the high imperial "oracle renaissance," the same surge of interest that filled Claros with city delegations. On that reading the dice oracles aren't a poor substitute for the famous sanctuaries. They're part of the same wave.

And there's something to be said for a system nobody could fix. A priest can shade an answer. A bone can't. The verses take the god behind the throw entirely seriously, and a few of them warn the reader against trying to force his hand. Nobody writes that warning into a game.

We don't know who actually stood at these pillars. The inscriptions preserve the answers and never the questions, and no ancient writer describes watching anyone use one. Whether a merchant at Kremna felt he was getting Apollo on the cheap, or getting him straight, is the one thing the stone doesn't say.

What happened to the dice

The bones themselves turn up everywhere. Museums hold them by the drawerful, real ones and copies in bronze, lead and glass, and they are almost impossible to date or assign to any purpose. The same object could be a toy, a gambling piece or a question for Zeus, depending on who was holding it and what they were hoping for.

The texts outlived the pillars. Lot oracles with fixed lists of answers carried on into the Christian centuries, in churches and manuscripts from the Negev to Gaul. The pillar at Kremna is the older version of the same idea: fifty-six numbered replies, carved in stone, waiting in the market for anybody with a handful of bones.
