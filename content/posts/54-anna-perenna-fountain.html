Somebody in late Roman Rome wanted a man called Sura to go blind.

They scratched the request onto a small sheet of lead. Take out his eyes, it says, the right one and the left one. It repeats the demand, in case the first time wasn't clear, and adds that Sura was born of an accursed womb. Then it went into the water.

Some sixteen centuries later, in October 1999, a crew digging an underground car park at Piazza Euclide in the Parioli district went through the roof of the place where it had landed.

A goddess with a drinking problem

What they'd hit was a fountain: a brick and tufa trough, with a cistern behind it built around a natural spring, between six and ten metres below the modern street. Three inscribed marbles had been built into the trough wall. Two of them name the owner. Nymphis sacratis Annae Perennae: to the consecrated nymphs of Anna Perenna.

Anna Perenna was already known, mostly from Ovid. Her festival was the Ides of March, and in the Fasti it's a picnic that gets out of hand. Couples sprawl on the grass by the Tiber, rig up tents from branches and togas, and pray for as many years of life as they can drink cups of wine. The girls sing songs Ovid describes as obscene.

The inscriptions from the fountain are more respectable. One is an altar set up by Suetonius Germanus and his wife Licinia, paying off a vow after a victory in some contest, and dated 5 April 156 CE. Another is in verse, from a freedman thanking the nymphs for his patron's win.

One awkwardness. A calendar from the reign of Tiberius puts the festival at the first milestone of the Via Flaminia. The fountain is between the second and third. T. P. Wiseman suggested the cult had moved. Marina Piranomonte, who ran the dig, says it never did: there is pottery of the fourth and third centuries BCE at the mouth of the spring.

What was in the mud

The spring kept running after people stopped coming, and laid clay and sand over everything in the cistern. That sealed it from the air. Piranomonte's list of what came out: 549 coins, 74 oil lamps, 22 curse tablets, 18 containers of lead sheet and three of clay, a copper cauldron scorched on the bottom, seven pine cones and some eggshells.

The coins run from Augustus to Theodosius. Most of the lamps had never been lit. Six had a little rolled lead tablet pushed into the nozzle where the wick should go, a curse in place of a flame.

We already had plenty of curse tablets thrown into springs and graves. The containers were the surprise.

Three tins, one inside another

Each lead container is really three: cylinders nested like Russian dolls and sealed with resin so they could never be opened. Six of them had a figurine inside.

The figurines are crude things, a head and shoulders tapering to a tail. Analysis found wax, flour, sugars, herbs and milk. X-rays found that each one was moulded around a sliver of animal bone. In two cases the bone had slipped out, and turned out to have writing on it.

One is worse than the rest. The doll is wrapped in a lead sheet with two nails driven through it, one of them through a face scratched on the lead. From under the sheet a snake comes up, about to bite the figure in the head.

The clay woman in the Louvre, with her thirteen pins, came out of Egypt with a recipe to explain her. These came with nothing. We have the object and the name of a target, when there is one, and have to guess at the grudge.

The rooster on the tin

Scratched on several of the inner canisters is a figure with a cock's head and human feet, with rows of Greek letters on its belly. Beside one of them is a name, Leontius.

Who is the rooster? A cock-headed god with snakes for legs is familiar from magical gems, where he usually travels with the name Abraxas. Writing in 2010, Piranomonte doubted the match. This one has no snake legs, and the gem figure is normally a protector. She thought a cock-headed demon of the kind drawn in one Egyptian spellbook, dragging a victim off, was the likelier model. Elsewhere, writing with Francisco Marco Simón, she calls it Abraxas outright.

Then there are the letters. They've since been read as initials: Jesus Christ of Nazareth, then God, God, God. If that's right, Jürgen Blänsdorf's conclusion follows, that whoever made these had cast Abraxas as the father of Christ, and was cursing in his name at a pagan nymph's spring.

It rests on reading a row of eleven letters as abbreviations. We don't know that it's right.

Whose fingerprints?

The resin on one container kept the prints of whoever pressed it shut. They were small. The police technicians who examined them inclined to think they were a woman's, and "the sorceress of Piazza Euclide" has had a healthy career in print ever since.

Piranomonte herself put the brakes on in 2010. The tablets are in several hands and several kinds of Latin, some nearly classical, some far gone. The canisters look like one skilled person's work, or one workshop's. The loose tablets and the lamps look like customers of all sorts, doing it the cheap way.

Blänsdorf dates most of the texts to the middle and later fourth century, with one perhaps a century or two older. The newest coin was struck between 388 and 393. Not long after that somebody filled the trough with smashed wine jars. It may have been the law, since Theodosius banned the old cults in 391 and 392. It may just have been a bad decade for the suburbs, with the Goths on the way.

The curse on Sura gives one more detail. He was an arbiter, a judge or referee. Blänsdorf knows of no other Latin curse aimed only at the eyes. Somebody thought Sura had seen something, or failed to.
