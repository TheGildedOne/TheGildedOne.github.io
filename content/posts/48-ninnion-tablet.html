The plaque is smaller than a dinner plate. Red clay, cracked and rejoined, kept behind glass in Room 15 of the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. On it: a line of men, women and children carrying torches and myrtle branches, walking toward a woman seated on a rock with grain in her lap. Above them, a frieze of moon discs. At the bottom edge, two words scratched by hand: a dedication from someone named Ninnion.

That is the whole object. It is also, as far as anyone has found in more than a century of digging at Eleusis, the only ancient picture that claims to show the Mysteries actually taking place.

A votive plaque, not a manual

Excavators pulled the tablet from the ground at Eleusis in 1895, broken and missing pieces, and reassembled it to something close to its original shape: 44.5 centimetres tall, 33 wide, the outline cut to suggest a small temple front, a naiskos. Four holes at the corners once let it hang, probably inside the sanctuary itself, left there by whoever it belonged to and never taken down.

The inscription along the base is short enough to translate in one line: Ninnion dedicated this to the two goddesses. That is everything the object tells us about her. No family name, no husband, no reason. Later writers have guessed she might have been a well-known figure in fourth-century Athens, but the tablet itself supports none of that. It supports two words and a picture.

Torches, a kernos, and a seated goddess

The lower register is the clearer of the two. A youth carrying torches, generally read as Iacchus, the daimon who led Eleusinian processions, walks ahead of a woman and a bearded man toward a seated female figure holding stalks of grain: almost certainly Demeter, waiting at the end of the road. The woman following Iacchus carries a flat, spouted vessel called a kernos balanced on her head, an object used elsewhere in Eleusinian ritual for carrying different kinds of seeds and offerings together. Scholars have suggested, without being able to prove it, that this woman is Ninnion herself, walking into her own dedication.

The upper register repeats the shape of a procession, more figures moving with torches and branches, but drops one thing the lower scene has: there is no clear appearance of Persephone. That absence is small, and it is the whole argument.

One visit, or two

Kevin Clinton, whose 1992 study remains the standard modern treatment of Eleusinian iconography, reads the two registers as different moments in the initiation calendar rather than one continuous crowd. The Athenians kept two related festivals: the Lesser Mysteries, held in spring at Agrai near Athens, and the Greater Mysteries, held in autumn at Eleusis itself, on the nineteenth of the month Boedromion. Persephone, on this reading, belongs to the spring rite at Agrai and is missing from the autumn scene at Eleusis for exactly that reason. The tablet, in other words, is not one snapshot but two, stacked because a small plaque had no better way to show a journey that took months.

Other scholars have preferred a simpler unity: one crowd, one occasion, drawn across two bands purely because the artist ran out of room in one. Nothing on the tablet settles it. There are no labels, no captions, nothing beyond the figures and their gestures, and every reading depends on matching those gestures against festival calendars and ritual objects known mostly from centuries-later sources. Mylonas, whose own excavations at the site shaped a generation of scholarship on the sanctuary, worked from the same image and reached his own reconstruction of the sequence, not identical to Clinton's. The disagreement has outlived both of their books.

What the tablet doesn't show

It is worth being blunt about the limit here. Whatever the Mysteries actually revealed inside the Telesterion, the great hall that could seat several thousand initiates at once, this tablet does not show it. Nothing does. Initiates who spoke about the rite at all, and most didn't, described the approach: the procession from Athens along the Sacred Way, the fasting, the drinking of the kykeon, the crowd. What happened once the doors of the hall closed was kept, by every account we have, and it was kept well enough that no ancient writer ever broke the silence in detail.

Ninnion's plaque sits exactly on that boundary. It shows the walk in, the torches, the goddess waiting at the end of the road. It stops precisely where the secret starts, which may be the most honest thing about it: even the one worshipper who put her own memory of the rite into permanent clay chose to draw the part she was allowed to.
