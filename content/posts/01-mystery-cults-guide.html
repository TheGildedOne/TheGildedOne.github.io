In 415 BCE a young Athenian aristocrat named Alcibiades allegedly did an impression of a priest at a dinner party. Within months Athens had recalled its most capable general from the largest military expedition in its history, condemned him to death in absentia, and driven him into the arms of Sparta.

The impression was of the Eleusinian Mysteries. Performing them outside the sanctuary, for laughs, in front of the uninitiated, was a capital offence — and the city took it seriously enough to wreck its own war effort over it.

That is the thing worth understanding about the mystery cults. Not that they were secret. That the secret held, for two thousand years, in a civilisation of enthusiastic gossips who wrote down absolutely everything else, including their laundry and their insults.

What a mystery cult actually was

The word misleads. Mystēria comes from myein — to close, as in the eyes or the mouth. An initiate was a mystēs: one who keeps shut. There's no puzzle implied, no riddle to solve. The name just means the things you don't talk about.

Four features set them apart from normal Greek and Roman religion:

  • You chose it. You were born into the cult of Athena. You applied to Eleusis.
  • There was a threshold. A before and an after, and a ceremony in between that changed your status permanently.
  • Talking was punished. Sometimes with death, as Alcibiades discovered.
  • It promised you something personally. Not good harvests for the city — something for you, usually concerning what happened after you died.

That last one is why anyone bothered. Homer's afterlife is grim: the dead are gibbering shades, and Achilles says outright he'd rather be a living farmhand than king of the corpses. The mysteries offered a different deal.

Eleusis, where everybody went

The Eleusinian Mysteries ran annually for something close to two millennia and were about as fringe as a national holiday. Athens administered them. Hadrian was initiated. Marcus Aurelius was initiated.

And so, remarkably, was almost anyone else who asked. The requirements were that you spoke enough Greek to follow along and hadn't committed an unpurified murder. Women qualified. Enslaved people qualified. Foreigners qualified. In a world built almost entirely on exclusion, Eleusis charged a modest fee and let in nearly everyone.

The build-up is well documented, because none of it was secret: a nine-day festival, a fast, a bath in the sea clutching a piglet, and a fourteen-mile torchlit procession during which masked strangers stood on a bridge and shouted obscenities at you.

Then several thousand exhausted, starving, thoroughly primed people filed into a vast dark hall called the Telesterion.

What happened next is the best-kept secret in European history. The evidence for what went on in that hall is thinner and stranger than most accounts admit.

Dionysus, and the cult Rome panicked about

In 186 BCE the Roman Senate decided that Bacchic worship was a conspiracy against the state and moved against it with startling violence. Livy, writing much later and clearly enjoying himself, describes nocturnal orgies, forged wills, poisonings and murder. Thousands were executed.

We don't have to take his word for it, which is the interesting part. The Senate's actual decree survives on a bronze tablet — and it is far more revealing than Livy's scandal-mongering. What it bans is not debauchery. It bans organisation: shared funds, common oaths, officers, and meetings of more than five people.

Rome wasn't frightened of ecstasy. Rome was frightened of a membership list it didn't control.

Mithras, the cult we can see and can't read

Mithraism is the one we have the most of and understand the least.

Hundreds of its temples have been excavated, from Syria to Hadrian's Wall — small, deliberately cave-like rooms with benches down each side, seating maybe thirty men. It was male-only, it travelled with the army, and it had seven ranks of membership: Raven, Bridegroom, Soldier, Lion, Persian, Sun-Runner, Father.

And in essentially every one of them, the same picture: a young man in a Phrygian cap kneeling on a bull, yanking its head back, knifing it in the neck. A dog and a snake drink the blood. A scorpion grips the bull's testicles. A raven watches.

Hundreds of copies. Not one line of explanation from anyone who believed it. The attempts to decode it range from ingenious to wishful.

Isis, and the man who almost told us

Isis travelled better than any other Egyptian deity, and by the imperial period her cult had a full initiation structure and sanctuaries from Alexandria to Britain.

It also produced the one thing missing everywhere else: an eyewitness account. In Book 11 of Apuleius's novel, the narrator Lucius is initiated, and describes everything — the dream summons, the ten days of abstinence, the ritual bath, the linen robes.

Then he stops dead. He tells the reader he'd say more if he could, that it would burn their ears to hear it, and that he won't. All he offers is a handful of images: he reached the edge of death, crossed a threshold, and saw the sun blazing at midnight.

It's fiction, so it isn't testimony. But Apuleius was a real initiate of several cults and said so at his own trial. He knew exactly what he was refusing to write — in a novel, where nothing was forcing him.

The Orphics, who left the instructions in the grave

One group did write things down, just not in books.

Around forty sheets of gold leaf have been recovered from burials across the Greek world, some no bigger than a thumbnail, folded and placed with the dead. They aren't prayers. They're directions: which spring to avoid, which to drink from, what to say when something stops you and asks who you are.

They are the closest thing we have to a leaked document — and they only leaked because they were buried.

Why nobody talked

Fear explains some of it. Athens really would execute you.

But fear is a thin explanation for two thousand years of compliance across dozens of jurisdictions, every social class, and hundreds of thousands of people, some of whom were emperors and none of whom needed the money.

The better answer may be Aristotle's. Initiates, he reportedly said, did not learn anything — they underwent something, and were put into a state.

If that's right, the ban on disclosure wasn't protecting a doctrine. It was describing the problem. Whatever happened in that hall may simply not have survived being put into sentences — and the people who went through it would have known that better than anyone.

Which leaves us where it left everyone else: outside, in the dark, watching the torches go in without us.
