In the spring of 1962, workmen digging foundations near the old railway station in Constanța, on Romania's Black Sea coast, hit something hard. Within a few hours they had pulled twenty-four marble sculptures out of the ground.

Someone in ancient Tomis had buried them in a hurry, but carefully, propped against each other so the earth wouldn't crush them. Whatever they were hiding from, they wanted these back. Among the gods and goddesses was a snake, coiled on itself in loops, with a head that is not quite a snake's. It has a muzzle like a lamb's, long flowing hair, and an ear like a man's.

His name was Glykon. If you believe the only writer who tells his story, he was a glove puppet.

How to make a god

That writer is Lucian of Samosata, the sharpest satirist of the second century CE, and his pamphlet Alexander or the False Prophet is a hatchet job from its first page. Its target is Alexander of Abonoteichos, a handsome man from a small town on the Black Sea coast of Paphlagonia, in what is now northern Turkey.

Lucian's version goes like this. Alexander bought a big, tame snake in Pella, in Macedonia. He buried bronze tablets in the temple of Apollo at Chalcedon announcing that Asclepius, the healing god, was about to move to Abonoteichos. When the people of his home town started building the god a temple, he hid a blown goose egg with a newborn snake inside it in the foundations, then "discovered" it in front of a crowd and cracked it open. Asclepius was born.

A few days later the baby was a full-grown serpent. Alexander kept the Pella snake's head tucked under his arm and showed off a painted linen head with an almost human face, its mouth opened and closed with horsehairs. An oracle in verse announced the new god's name. Glykon, "the sweet one", grandson of Zeus.

Then came the business model. Questions were handed in sealed. Alexander opened them with a heated needle, read them, resealed them and returned them with answers attached. The fee, Lucian says, was a drachma and two obols per oracle. For rich clients there were "autophones": answers spoken by Glykon himself, through cranes' windpipes threaded into the linen head with a man speaking from the next room.

It's a wonderful story, and it's all Lucian. Nobody else describes the egg, the puppet or the needle. He had, he tells us, met Alexander, and he hated him.

The narrator with a grudge

The pamphlet's best scenes are about Lucian himself. Granted an audience, he says, he was offered Alexander's right hand to kiss, and bit it instead, hard. Alexander smiled and let it go, then lent him a ship for the voyage home, and the crew were under orders to throw him overboard. The captain, in tears, refused, and put him ashore.

We have only Lucian's word for any of that. Peter Thonemann, whose 2021 edition is the fullest modern study, warns repeatedly against treating the pamphlet's "Lucian" as a reliable witness just because he shares the author's name. The narrator is a character in a satire, and satire does not footnote.

Thonemann's Alexander is a different man: a member of his town's elite who built a cult with real civic standing, and an oracle that worked like other oracles of the day. Claros, the most respected oracle in Asia Minor at the time, also answered in verse. Jan Bremmer, reviewing the edition, thought he let the pendulum swing too far, and that Alexander really was an exceptional operator. Whether Alexander believed in his god is a question neither of them can answer. Nobody can.

What the stones say

Here's what isn't in doubt. Somebody important was listening. Lucian says the Roman senator Rutilianus, a former consul, became the oracle's chief patron and, in his sixties, married Alexander's daughter on the god's instruction. When the Marcomannic war broke out on the Danube, an oracle told the army to throw two live lions into the river. They swam across, and the enemy clubbed them to death as strange dogs.

During the great plague of the 160s, Glykon issued a one-line verse to protect households: "Phoebus, the god unshorn, keeps off the cloud of plague." Lucian sneers that the houses that wrote it over their doors were the ones that emptied. Satire, maybe. But the same line has turned up carved on an inscription at Antioch, hundreds of miles away, which means at least one person there took it seriously.

Then there are the coins. Lucian says that near the end of his life Alexander asked the emperor to rename Abonoteichos as Ionopolis, and to strike coins with Glykon on one side. That sounds like more mockery, except that it happened. Coins of Antoninus Pius already show the long-haired snake with the town's name. Later issues from Ionopolis show him too, including one under Severus Alexander, in the 220s or 230s, where the city goddess sits feeding him from a dish. The town on that coast is still called İnebolu.

Dedications, statuettes and coins naming Glykon have been found from the Danube to the Euphrates. Daniel Ogden, in his big study of ancient serpent cults, puts him in a long tradition of snake gods linked with Asclepius, the same god whose snakes slid over sleepers at Epidaurus. Glykon wasn't a freak. He was a new face on a very old idea.

The god outlives the prophet

Alexander's own oracle promised him 150 years, and death by lightning. Lucian says he died before he was seventy, of a gangrenous leg crawling with maggots. It's the ending a satirist would choose, and like most of the pamphlet we can't check it.

We can check what happened next. The cult didn't collapse with its founder. Ionopolis was still advertising its snake on coins decades later, and Glykon's worshippers were still leaving dedications in the Balkans, where a statue of him was worth burying to keep safe.

Lucian wrote his pamphlet, he says, to show that a fraud had been a fraud. It's the reason anyone remembers Alexander's name.

The marble Glykon from Tomis, the one the workmen found in 1962, ended up on a Romanian banknote.
