Theogenes found a gemstone, a hyacinth, and brought it home. It sat in the house until it was stolen.

His wife Syntyche came under suspicion and was questioned. So she prayed to the god Men Axiottenos to clear her name. The stone came back. It was burnt and spoiled, wrapped in a linen shirt and left where it had been taken from. The thief was a girl called Apphia, daughter of Glykon, and on the thirty-first day the god destroyed her.

That would do for a miracle story. But the marble slab that tells it, a metre tall with a crescent moon carved at the top, wasn't put up to celebrate. Syntyche was made to put it up as a punishment. Apphia's mother had begged her to say nothing, and she'd agreed. The god was angry at the silence. She had put people before him, the stone says.

It's in the museum at Manisa in western Turkey, and it is one of a peculiar family.

Sin, sickness, stone

They come from the hill villages of Lydia and Phrygia, mostly a volcanic district the Greeks called the Burnt Land. The dated ones run from 114/5 to 263/4 CE. When Franz Steinleitner first studied them in 1913 he had twenty-one. By the 1980s there were over eighty. Georg Petzl's collection of 1994 has 124, and they keep turning up.

The plot rarely changes. Somebody does wrong. Something terrible follows. They work out, or are told, which god is responsible. They make amends, and the god requires the story in writing.

Aurelius Stratoneikos cut down trees without knowing they belonged to Zeus Sabazios and Artemis Anaitis. Antonia walked onto Apollo's land in dirty clothes. Meidon gave a dinner in a shrine where his servants ate meat that hadn't been sacrificed, and was struck dumb for three months. A woman called Trophime was summoned to serve the god, didn't come quickly enough, and was driven mad.

Some stelai add a carving of the body part the god struck, a pair of eyes for instance. The healing shrine at Epidaurus put its cures on stone for visitors to read. These villages put up their punishments.

Theodoros and his three offences

The longest and oddest was set up in 235/6 CE by a man named Theodoros, and it reads like the minutes of a hearing. Theodoros speaks, then Zeus, in turn.

Theodoros admits to sex with Trophime, another man's slave. Zeus names the price: a sheep, a partridge and a mole. A second offence, with a flute-girl, while Theodoros was himself a sacred slave: a piglet, a tuna, another fish. A third: a chicken, a sparrow, a pigeon, plus grain and wine for the priests.

Zeus then announces that he blinded Theodoros for all this, that the man has now appeased the gods, and that the prison may be opened. He is free after a year and ten months.

Whether that prison had walls, the stone doesn't say. And a god doesn't speak in the first person without someone to do the speaking. Somebody at that sanctuary was giving Zeus his lines.

The sceptre in the temple

The gods also worked as a court of last resort. If you'd been robbed, or accused, you could go to the sanctuary and "set up a sceptre": hand the matter over to the god, with curses on whoever was guilty.

That could go badly. A man called Jucundus went mad, and the village said his mother-in-law Tatias had drugged him. It's the same world of sideways suspicion that produced the evil eye. Tatias set up a sceptre and laid curses in the temple to prove her innocence. The stone says she knew she was guilty. She was punished. Then her son Sokrates dropped a vine-pruning sickle on his foot by the sacred grove and was dead within the day.

It was her grandchildren who had the sceptre lifted and the whole business carved, in 156 CE.

Angelos Chaniotis reads these texts as divine justice at work in village life. It's close kin to the binding spells Greeks aimed at opponents in court, with one difference. A curse tablet was folded and buried. This was done in front of the whole village and then put on marble.

Is "confession" the right word?

The name is a translation of the German Beichtinschriften, and almost nobody is happy with it.

"Confession" makes you picture a conscience. What the stones dwell on is power. Syntyche hadn't stolen anything. Her offence was failing to make the god's deed known. Several texts end by praising him or warning the reader not to take him lightly, and some call themselves a praise. Aslak Rostad proposed "reconciliation inscriptions" in 2002; "propitiatory" is now common. Petzl and Hasan Malay, publishing Syntyche's stone, used "confession or propitiatory" and left it there.

Behind the label is a harder question: whose voice is this? One reading has frightened villagers telling their own stories. Another has priests diagnosing the sick, setting the fee and dictating the text, with the stele as an advertisement for a temple you'd be unwise to cross. Eckhard Schnabel suggested the priests were tightening their grip because Christianity was spreading through the same valleys. He offers it as a suggestion, and no more than that.

Richard Gordon's caution is that there's no single model. Stones from one sanctuary resemble each other closely. Across the region they share only a family likeness.

We don't know how much of any text the sinner chose. Syntyche's ends on a detail nobody has explained. It records that when she set it up, her son Herakleides was thirteen. Her editors wonder if the boy was what the god had threatened.
