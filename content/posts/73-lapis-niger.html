The stone is a stump of grey tufa, a square pillar snapped off near the base. Letters run up one face and down the next, turning at the end of each line like an ox at the end of a furrow. Somebody broke it on purpose, packed gravel round it, and walked away.

It's the oldest public inscription in Latin from Rome, carved some time in the sixth century BCE. Nobody can fully read it. It seems to curse whoever disturbs the place, and it mentions a king.

A place of death

By the first century BCE, the spot was covered by a square of black marble paving in the Comitium, the old assembly ground in front of the Senate house. The Romans called it the lapis niger, the black stone, and they knew something lay beneath it. They didn't agree what.

Festus, summarising the Augustan scholar Verrius Flaccus, says the black stone marks a locus funestus, a place of death. It was meant for Romulus' burial, he says, but he wasn't buried there. Instead it held his foster-father Faustulus, or, others said, Hostus Hostilius, grandfather of King Tullus. Festus survives in one damaged manuscript, and both of those names are partly restored by modern editors.

Dionysius of Halicarnassus says Faustulus lay under a stone lion near the speakers' platform, and that Hostilius was buried "in the principal part of the Forum" under an inscription recording his valour. Varro, quoted by a later commentator on Horace, put Romulus' own tomb behind the platform. Plutarch reports a darker version: that the senators murdered Romulus in the shrine of Vulcan and carried him out in pieces under their robes.

So the Romans agreed the place was important and grim, and disagreed about whose it was. Horace, writing in the civil wars, imagines a barbarian conqueror scattering "the bones of Quirinus", Romulus deified, which it is sacrilege even to look at.

What was underneath

In January 1899 the archaeologist Giacomo Boni cleared the black paving and started digging below it. Within months he'd found the shrine the stories were about.

Under the marble lay a U-shaped altar with two plinths beside it, the sort that could carry a pair of lions. There was the stump of a stone cone or column, and the inscribed pillar. Around and over them lay a thick layer of burnt debris full of offerings: pottery, bronzes, animal bones, and a fragment of an Athenian vase painted with Hephaestus, the Greek Vulcan.

Boni thought the monument had been deliberately wrecked. The pillar is broken, the column truncated, the plinths damaged. Then the whole thing was sealed with gravel, and later still the black marble went down on top, most likely during the rebuilding of the Comitium and the Senate house in the 40s BCE.

Filippo Coarelli identified the shrine as the Volcanal, the ancient precinct of Vulcan "above the Comitium" that Roman writers mention, which would square with Plutarch's story and with the Hephaestus vase. Tim Cornell accepts it. Others point out that the vase fragment could have come in with a much later fill, and that Pliny writes of a lotus tree still growing in the Volcanal in his own day, which is awkward for a shrine buried under marble.

The king in the curse

The inscription has lost its top and therefore the start of every line, so what survives is a run of fragments. A few words are clear. Sakros esed: "he shall be sacer", accursed, handed over to the gods and outside human protection. Recei: "to the king". Kalatorem: a herald. Iouxmenta: yoked animals.

Most scholars read it as a sacred law protecting the spot. Beyond that there's little agreement. Robert Palmer built a whole book on the stone as a record of the king's role in the assembly. Georges Dumézil linked the yoked animals to a rule of Roman augury, that an omen was spoiled if a team in harness relieved itself while the auspices were being taken. That tells you how little text there is to go on.

Then there's the king. If the pillar dates from around 570 to 550 BCE, it was cut while Rome still had kings, and recei would be the only contemporary written trace of one. But the date rests on letter forms and the finds around it, and Gary Forsythe notes that recei could equally mean the rex sacrorum, the "king of sacred things", a priest the Republic kept on to perform the old royal rites after the kings were gone. The same word, two different Romes.

Buried on purpose

Rome was oddly selective with the remains of its own beginnings. The pontiffs' year-by-year record of the city was supposedly collected into eighty books and then lost, and in 181 BCE the Senate burned King Numa's books but let his bones stay in the ground. Here, whoever laid the black stone knew there was a shrine below and chose to mark it rather than dig it up.

The story still pulls. In 2020, archaeologists working beneath the steps of the Senate house a few metres away announced a sixth-century tufa box and a cylindrical block, and the headlines called it the tomb of Romulus. The site's director, Alfonsina Russo, said it wasn't.
