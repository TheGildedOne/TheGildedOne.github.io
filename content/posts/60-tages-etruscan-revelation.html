A man was ploughing a field near Tarquinia, and the blade went deeper than usual.

Something came up out of the furrow. It had the face of a child and, Cicero says, the good sense of an old man. It spoke. The ploughman shouted, people came running, and before long the whole of Etruria was standing round a hole in a field.

Its name was Tages. He talked at length, and the crowd wrote down every word. What he dictated was the art of the haruspex: how to read the will of the gods in the liver of a sacrificed animal. The Etruscans, Cicero says, kept the story in their own records as the beginning of their discipline.

Then he spends a paragraph taking it to pieces.

An augur's joke

The passage is in the second book of On Divination, written in 44 BCE. Book one puts the case for divination in the mouth of Cicero's brother Quintus. Book two is Cicero's reply, and it isn't kind.

Who could be so foolish, he asks, as to believe anyone ever ploughed up a god, or a man? If Tages was a god, why was he hiding underground waiting for a plough? If he was a man, how did he survive buried in the earth, and where did he learn what he taught? Cicero ends by admitting that arguing with the story makes him look sillier than the people who believe it.

Then he borrows Cato's old line. Cato used to wonder how one haruspex could look at another without laughing.

It lands harder when you know who's telling it. Cicero was himself an augur, one of Rome's official readers of signs, and had been since 53 BCE. The haruspices were a different profession, Etruscan specialists the senate hired when a prodigy needed explaining. Cicero could mock their craft without mocking his own. Mostly.

Later sightings

Cicero gives the earliest full account. Later writers add things he doesn't have.

Ovid puts the story in the Metamorphoses as one marvel in a list: a Tyrrhenian ploughman saw a clod of earth move by itself, take human shape, and open its mouth to tell the future. Festus, the dictionary-maker, makes Tages the son of a Genius and the grandson of Jupiter.

The detail people usually remember, that the child died the same day, is the latest of all. It comes from Isidore of Seville, a bishop writing in the seventh century CE, some seven hundred years after Cicero. Cicero doesn't say what became of Tages. He just stops being in the story.

John Lydus, a civil servant in sixth-century Constantinople, knew a different version. He describes a book in which Tarchon, the legendary founder of Tarquinia, puts questions and Tages answers them. Here Tarchon isn't a frightened farmer. He's a haruspex interviewing his teacher. Lydus says the original was written in old letters he found hard to make out, so he worked from translations.

The boy on the mirror

There's Etruscan evidence too, and it doesn't quite match the Roman story.

A bronze mirror from Tuscania, now in the archaeological museum in Florence, has a scene engraved on its back, usually dated to the third century BCE. A young man in a pointed priest's hat bends over a liver held in his left hand. A bearded older man leans on a staff and watches. Both are labelled. The older man is Avl Tarchunus. The younger is Pava Tarchies.

In 1930 Massimo Pallottino proposed that pava means "boy". So we have a boy diviner reading a liver in front of a man called Tarchon. It's tempting to see Tages instructing the founder. It's just as possible that this is Tarchon's son learning the trade. Nancy de Grummond has gathered other images that might belong to the story: engraved gems showing a bearded man bending over a figure rising from the ground, and a bronze statuette from Tarquinia of a seated infant with an adult's face.

None of them is labelled Tages. Whether the Etruscans told his story the way Cicero heard it, we don't know.

A liver older than Etruria

Here's the awkward part. The story says the discipline came out of Etruscan soil, complete, in a single afternoon. The objects say it travelled.

Reading the liver of a sacrificed sheep was a Mesopotamian science long before there were Etruscans. Clay models of livers, marked up and inscribed for teaching, survive from Mari on the Euphrates from around 1800 BCE, and from Babylonia, and from the Hittite capital in Anatolia. The bronze liver from Piacenza, the Etruscan equivalent, divides its surface into regions and gives each a god. Walter Burkert argued that the craft came west with travelling specialists, seers and healers who carried their skills from one court to the next.

That doesn't mean the Etruscans borrowed the whole thing. Their discipline grew features of its own, the doctrine of lightning above all, and Jean Turfa has shown how closely the one surviving Etruscan thunder calendar fits Italian weather and an Italian farming year. But the art had a past, and the origin story covers it over.

Italy seems to have liked its sacred knowledge to come out of the ground. Rome had the books of its king Numa, found by a man ploughing in 181 BCE. And the Piacenza liver itself was turned up by a farmer behind a plough, in 1877.

What the crowd wrote down

The notes became the libri Tagetici, the books of Tages. Roman writers cite them alongside other Etruscan books on lightning and ritual. None survives. What we have of the discipline comes in quotations, Latin paraphrases, and that thunder calendar, preserved in Greek by Lydus after passing through a Latin translation of an Etruscan original.

Lydus is the last ancient writer we know of who had the Tages material in front of him. He says the letters were too old to make out, so he read the translations.
