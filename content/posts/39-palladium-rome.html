In 241 BCE the temple of Vesta caught fire, and the chief priest of Rome stopped at the door.

Lucius Caecilius Metellus was the pontifex maximus, the one man in the city allowed inside Vesta's temple at all. He was still not allowed into the room he now needed to enter. So he drew water, lifted his hands, and said the words Ovid later put in his mouth: forgive me, holy things, a man is going where no man may go. Then he went in and carried out an object that almost nobody in Rome had ever been permitted to look at.

The thing in the back room

The Palladium was small and old, wooden by most accounts, an armed figure generally identified as Pallas Athena. It stood in the penus Vestae, the storeroom at the back of Vesta's round temple in the Forum, behind a curtain, in the dark. The six Vestal Virgins could go in. The pontifex maximus could go in. That was the whole list.

Rome kept a class of objects it called the pignora imperii, the pledges of empire: things the city's continued existence was said to rest on. Ancient writers give slightly different contents for the set, but the Palladium is on every version of the list, and usually at the top of it. The state guarded sacred knowledge the way it guarded a frontier. The only comparable collection, the Sibylline Books, could be consulted only after a vote of the Senate and only by the priests assigned to it. Sixty years after the fire, that same body would order the books found in the supposed grave of king Numa burned as a threat to Roman religion.

Because seeing the Palladium was forbidden, nobody could describe it. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, writing under Augustus, says plainly that the holy things in the Vestals' keeping are known to no one but the priestesses, and that he will not guess. He then passes on a story that a man who once forced his way in during a fire lost his sight for it.

Carried from Troy, or stolen before it fell

The origin story ran through Troy. The Palladium had fallen from the sky to the city's founder, and while it stood inside the walls, Troy could not be taken. Which is why, in the Greek telling, Odysseus and Diomedes creep in and steal it before the sack, and Virgil has a character say the Greeks' whole hope hung on that theft.

That left Rome with a problem, because Rome needed the real Palladium to have survived Troy and travelled west with Aeneas. The fix was to have two. Diomedes took a copy, or took the real one and later handed it over, while Aeneas brought the genuine article to Italy, where it eventually reached Vesta's hearth. Dionysius records the two-Palladia tradition and cannot resolve it. The single most important object in Rome came with a provenance the Romans themselves told at least two incompatible ways.

What the fire cost him

Ovid's version of 241 is a rescue with a happy ending. Metellus bursts in among the Vestals, tells them tears are no use, and shouts at them to pick up the pledges and carry them out. He draws water, apologises to the goddess, enters the forbidden room, and brings her out. Nothing bad happens to him. His devotion saves the relic, and the goddess, Ovid says, approved.

Pliny tells it as a disaster. In the Natural History he writes that this Metellus passed an old age of blindness, having lost his sight in a fire while saving the Palladium from the temple of Vesta. He offers it as the one dark thread in an otherwise golden life. Valerius Maximus repeats the blinding too.

So the sources cannot agree on the basic outcome. Same afternoon, same man, same burning room: divine favour and no mark on him, or his eyesight for the rest of his life.

The speech that left it out

There is a reason to doubt the blindness. Pliny himself, a few lines earlier, quotes the eulogy that Metellus's son delivered at his funeral, which lists the ten greatest things the old man achieved: to be a first-rate warrior, a supreme orator, a brave general, to conduct the greatest campaigns under his own command, to hold the highest offices, to be supremely wise, to be the foremost senator, to acquire a great fortune honestly, to leave many children, and to be the most distinguished man in the state. A son assembling a boast that specific would have used a blinding suffered for the goddess, if it had happened and if it did the family credit. It is not there. The blindness is Pliny's own addition, tacked on straight after.

There is a practical snag as well. In 224 BCE, seventeen years after the fire, Metellus was made dictator to hold the elections. Handing that job to a man who had been blind since 241 would be a strange choice. Most modern historians read the blinding as a later embellishment: a good story that attached itself to a priest whose family never stopped advertising its ancestors. The Caecilii Metelli put the elephants this same Metellus had captured from Carthage on their coins for more than a century. A relative who walked into fire for Vesta was not going to be allowed to fade.

Back behind the curtain

Whatever it cost, the Palladium went straight back into the dark and stayed there. Around 220 CE the emperor Elagabalus had it moved to a new temple so he could marry it to his Syrian sun god, and then it was returned. When the temple of Vesta was shut for good at the end of the fourth century, the Palladium drops out of the record entirely.

A late story says Constantine had it buried under the porphyry column he raised in his new forum at Constantinople, so that it would guard a Christian capital the way it had guarded a pagan one. Nobody has ever dug to find out. The column is still standing, cracked and bound with iron hoops, in the middle of a traffic junction in Istanbul.
